## D.0 How to read this annex

This annex is doctrinal and strategic analysis for the manuscript's reviewability argument. It is
not advocacy, not body text, and not legal advice. Standing, finality, reviewability, and remedy
require independent legal judgment, which this annex does not resolve. The
analysis deliberately surfaces the weaknesses in the standing theory alongside its strengths,
because the manuscript's claim is that the petition path is cleaner than direct review, not that
standing is assured.

## D.1 The threshold point: filing needs no standing

Article III standing is irrelevant to the petition itself. Section 553(e) confers on "an interested
person" the right to petition for issuance of a rule, and 24 C.F.R. § 10.20 implements that right
for HUD. Filing the petition, and preparing or later submitting a PRA comment on the June 12, 2026 renewal notice, are
acts that require no standing. Section 555(e) (and § 10.20) then obligate HUD to give prompt notice
of any denial accompanied by a brief statement of the grounds. Standing doctrine applies only at
the next stage, when HUD denies (or unreasonably delays) and a plaintiff seeks § 706 judicial
review. This sequencing is favorable: the predicate-creating act has no standing cost, and the
standing question is deferred to litigation that may never be necessary.

## D.2 Extended ADAPT analysis

*ADAPT v. HUD*, 170 F.3d 381 (3d Cir. 1999), and the district court's underlying opinion, 1998 WL
113802, bear adverse weight on reviewability. The Note's claim is distinguishable on three grounds,
developed here in full.

**Ground 1 — discrete rulemaking petition, not generalized enforcement compulsion.** ADAPT sought
to compel HUD to undertake generalized disability enforcement. The Third Circuit's holding that HUD
"has no affirmative duty under any statute or regulation to initiate nationwide investigation and
prosecution," 170 F.3d at 386, is addressed to enforcement discretion under *Heckler v. Chaney*,
470 U.S. 821 (1985). The present claim targets a discrete rulemaking petition under § 553(e) and
24 C.F.R. § 10.20, asking for a reasoned disposition of a specific request grounded in specific
statutory and regulatory text. *Massachusetts v. EPA*, 549 U.S. 497, 527-28 (2007), confirms that
denial of a rulemaking petition is reviewable under the arbitrary-and-capricious standard even
where enforcement discretion might be unreviewable. The enforcement-versus-rulemaking line is the
distinction *Chaney* itself draws.

**Ground 2 — the statutory-scope point ADAPT actually decided.** ADAPT pressed § 3608(e)(6) and,
below, Part 121 only as "law to apply" against discretionary enforcement. The Third Circuit
rejected APA review on that *Chaney* axis while construing § 3608(e)(6) as concerning data on those
who benefit or seek to benefit from HUD programs, 170 F.3d at 387 n.9 — the person-level scope this
Note adopts. Section 3614a was never invoked in ADAPT. The district court's one-sentence rejection
of Part 121 misdescribed the regulation as implementing the FHAA's accessibility requirements;
neither opinion engaged Part 121's actual text (24 C.F.R. § 121.2, "Collection of Data"). The Note
therefore does not contradict ADAPT's actual holding: it adopts ADAPT's person-level reading of
§ 3608(e)(6), invokes § 3614a (which ADAPT never addressed), and rests on a reading of Part 121
that neither ADAPT opinion reached.

**Ground 3 — the § 3604(f)(5)(D) plan-review bar does not reach the proposal.** Section
3604(f)(5)(D) bars requiring the Secretary to "review or approve the plans, designs or construction
of all covered multifamily dwellings." That provision addresses federal plan-review of private
construction. The proposed collection concerns records of federally assisted programs whose
recipients already carry § 504 and Part 8 duties under their funding instruments — not plan-review
of private construction. The bar is therefore inapposite.

**The record that did not exist in 1998.** Finally, the 2022-2026 PRA record — in which HUD
invoked, narrowed, and then declined to carry forward a Part 121-adjacent collection — did not
exist when ADAPT was decided. The combination the Note relies on (express statutory data authority
under § 3614a, regulatory text HUD itself promulgated, the 2022-2026 administrative record, and
§ 504/Part 8 duties that presuppose the records sought) is one ADAPT never presented. (This
develops the manuscript's footnote 122.)

## D.3 Protection and Advocacy statutory-function detail

The manuscript's Part IV.B states that a state P&A agency can anchor an organizational layer only
through a concrete impairment of its statutory monitoring and rights-protection functions, not
through a generalized data interest. The statutory basis is as follows.

The P&A statutes are the Developmental Disabilities Assistance and Bill of Rights Act, 42 U.S.C.
§ 15041 *et seq.*; the Protection and Advocacy for Individuals with Mental Illness Act, 42 U.S.C.
§ 10801 *et seq.*; and the Protection and Advocacy of Individual Rights program, 29 U.S.C. § 794e.
P&A systems hold statutory investigation, remedial, records-access, and information-and-referral
authorities. See 42 U.S.C. § 15043(a)(2); 42 U.S.C. § 10805(a); 29 U.S.C. § 794e(f).

Standing on this basis requires a concrete impairment of the exercise of those enumerated functions
in housing-related representation — not a generalized interest in disability data. The organizing
authority is *Havens Realty Corp. v. Coleman*, 455 U.S. 363, 379 (1982), which recognized
organizational standing where the defendant's conduct impairs the organization's own activities.
*Havens* must be read narrowly after *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, 602 U.S. 367
(2024), which recharacterizes *Havens* as involving conduct that "directly affected and interfered
with" the organization's "core business activities," 602 U.S. at 395. A P&A agency's standing
theory therefore has to plead a concrete impairment of a specific statutory monitoring or
rights-protection function that operates directly on the disability-related housing information
§ 121.2 identifies and the 2023 and 2026 collections omit — closer to *Havens* operational
impairment than to a generalized data interest. Even so pleaded, the theory remains subordinate to
naming an individual, for the reasons in § D.4. (This develops the institutional-standing layer
the manuscript's Part IV.B now compresses; the associational-standing framework and the P&A
statutory predicate are addressed further in the LIMITATIONS section.)

## D.4 Individual-petitioner standing architecture

The manuscript's Part IV.B leads with an individual disabled HUD-program participant or applicant
and runs the claim on the procedural-rights track. The architecture has four elements.

**Injury.** The lead petitioner's injury is a concrete, ongoing, prospective stake in HUD's lawful
administration of the assisted-housing programs on which the petitioner's housing depends — framed
as live dependence on functions HUD administers, not as informational deprivation. After
*TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 594 U.S. 413 (2021), informational deprivation without downstream
concrete consequence is insufficient, so the injury is framed as the petitioner's concrete,
ongoing interest in the accessible-unit, accommodation, and transfer functions on which her housing
depends. The petitioner must be genuinely live: a voucher holder, a pending waitlist applicant for
an accessible unit, or a tenant with an open accommodation, modification, or transfer request,
ideally tied to a specific enumerated Part 8 feature (24 C.F.R. §§ 8.22, 8.26, 8.27).

**Traceability via procedural default.** Ordinary but-for causation is difficult: HUD's missing
records lead to a housing provider's poorer records, which make a future adjudication harder — a
multi-link, third-party-dependent chain of the kind *Alliance* is built to defeat. The design
therefore locates the injury in HUD's own procedural default — the denial of reasoned
consideration, which runs from HUD to the petitioner in one step — and rests on the procedural-
rights track. Under *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 572 n.7 (1992), a plaintiff
vindicating a procedural right that protects concrete interests may challenge the agency's failure
to observe it "without meeting all the normal standards for redressability and immediacy." The
§ 553(e) and § 555(e) reasoned-response right is the procedure; the petitioner's live housing
dependence is the concrete interest that anchors it.

**The *Alliance* channel.** *Alliance* makes standing "substantially more difficult" where a
plaintiff challenges the government's regulation, or lack of regulation, of someone else, 602 U.S.
at 382. The response is that the challenged action is HUD's denial of the petitioner's own § 553(e)
petition — a duty running to the petitioner personally under §§ 553(e) and 555(e) — so the claim
does not depend on predicting the choices of housing providers not before the court. *Alliance*
itself points objectors to precisely this channel, 602 U.S. at 393 (parties "may always take their
concerns to the Executive" and seek greater regulation).

**The CoreLogic first-step tie.** *Connecticut Fair Housing Center v. CoreLogic Rental Property
Solutions, LLC*, Nos. 23-1118(L), 23-1166(XAP) (2d Cir. Feb. 20, 2026), dismissed a fair-housing
organization's appeal for lack of standing after *Alliance* while reaching the individual
co-plaintiff's FHA claim on the merits. That asymmetry supports leading with the individual. But
candor requires the adverse half as well: the court affirmed against the individual on
proximate-cause grounds, holding that liability "will not extend beyond the first step" of the
causal chain. That holding is the reason the petition locates the injury one step from HUD — the
denial of reasoned consideration — rather than in downstream housing outcomes. The lead theory must
be defended against CoreLogic's proximate-cause holding by distinguishing its FHA-merits posture
from the APA procedural-rights posture here, not by assuming around it.

**Participation is posture, not interest.** Participation alone — the author's prepared or later-filed comment or a § 553(e)
petition — supplies the procedural posture, not the concrete Article III interest, which must come
from the petitioner's own assisted-housing transaction. Any later-filed comment would be record
evidence, not a standing predicate. (This consolidates the manuscript's footnotes 149, 150, and
152.)

**Relief.** The relief sought is correspondingly modest: a nontrivial opportunity to obtain reasoned
agency consideration of the records HUD's own duties presuppose — not a guarantee that any
particular collection will issue or that any past denial will be undone. Review proceeds under
§ 706(2)(A) with remand under *Florida Power & Light Co. v. Lorion*, 470 U.S. 729, 744 (1985), not
as a § 706(1) command to collect data, which *Norton v. Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance*, 542
U.S. 55 (2004), would bar because § 3614a's "may make rules" is discretionary.

## D.5 Anticipated objection and response

This section states the strongest standing objection in the form a government litigant would press
it, then the response the Note adopts. It is anticipated-objection analysis; it does not concede
the objection.

**The objection.** A reviewing court could frame the standing challenge as follows. The plaintiff's
concrete housing injury, if any, is traceable to a housing provider — a PHA, owner, or property
manager — that denied an accommodation, transfer, accessible-unit placement, or voucher-related
request. HUD's alleged failure to collect disability data did not deny the plaintiff housing and
did not alter the legal obligations of the third party who did. Filing a § 553(e) petition does not
manufacture injury; a petitioner cannot create standing by asking an agency to regulate third
parties and then suing when the agency declines. *Lujan* footnote 7 relaxes redressability and
immediacy only for a plaintiff whose concrete interest is already threatened by agency action
subject to a required procedure; it does not eliminate causation, and *Summers v. Earth Island
Institute*, 555 U.S. 488 (2009), forecloses procedural-right standing untethered to a concrete
application. *TransUnion* bars reframing a generalized informational grievance as concrete injury,
and *Alliance* confirms that dissatisfaction with agency regulation of others belongs in the
Executive Branch. At most, the plaintiff seeks better HUD administration that might someday
influence future regulated-party behavior, which is too speculative.

**The response.** The response does not deny that the proximate housing decision comes from a
provider; it relocates the injury. The reviewable action is HUD's denial of the petitioner's own
§ 553(e) petition — a duty that runs to the petitioner personally under §§ 553(e) and 555(e) — so
the claim does not turn on predicting the provider's choices. The concrete interest that anchors
the *Lujan* footnote 7 procedure is the petitioner's live dependence on the assisted-housing
functions (accessible-unit placement, accommodation, transfer) on which her housing turns; the
open transaction matters not because a disability-data rule would necessarily resolve it, but
because it identifies the concrete assisted-housing function the required procedure protects. That
framing answers *Summers*, which forecloses only a procedural right asserted "in the abstract": the
right here is tethered to a concrete, current application. It answers *TransUnion* by resting on a
concrete housing interest rather than a bare interest in data. And it takes up the channel
*Alliance* itself identifies for objectors who seek greater regulation — a petition to the agency,
followed by review of a denial — rather than a challenge to the regulation of third parties.

**What the response does not claim.** The response lowers the standing risk; it does not eliminate
it. Two links remain genuinely contestable and are flagged as requiring independent legal
judgment: (1) the *Summers* nexus — whether a court accepts that the § 10.20 / § 553(e)
reasoned-response duty protects the individual applicant's concrete housing interest rather than a
generalized interest in agency data; and (2) the concreteness of the data-omission injury itself
under *TransUnion* — whether "live dependence on the program" is a concrete downstream harm from
the omission or a repackaged procedural injury. The CoreLogic proximate-cause holding can be
deployed against both, and the FHA-merits/APA-procedural distinction that answers it is real but
contestable. The honest position is that the individual-lead procedural-rights theory is the
strongest available configuration, not that it is assured.

## D.6 Additional Standing and Reviewability Issues (cited by fns 119 and 121)

**The *Alliance* information-collection aside (fn 119).** *FDA v. Alliance for
Hippocratic Medicine*, 602 U.S. 367 (2024), also rejected, in passing, an
argument that the FDA was "not properly collecting and disseminating
information," where the plaintiffs identified no statute requiring collection or
dissemination. 602 U.S. at 395–96. The petition configuration differs on both
scores: §§ 3608(e)(6) and 121.2 supply the legal duty, and the lead petitioner's
injury is her own program access, not informational deprivation in the abstract
(see D.4).

**The author's PRA comment (fn 121).** The author's as-filed PRA comment
(manuscript note 153) is record evidence in the administrative file, not a
standing predicate; no standing theory in this annex rests on it.

119 and 121; the manuscript retains the holdings, the pincites, and the

## LIMITATIONS

- **Independent legal judgment required.** This is research analysis; the questions of standing,
  finality, reviewability, and remedy are not resolved here. The ratings and characterizations are
  analytic, not conclusions of law.
- **Individual-lead theory is MODERATE, not assured.** The strongest configuration — a live,
  feature-specific individual disabled participant on the *Lujan* footnote 7 procedural-rights
  track — is contestable on the *Summers* nexus, on *TransUnion* concreteness, and against
  CoreLogic's in-circuit 2026 proximate-cause holding. Every theory that does not run through a
  live individual interest is weaker after *TransUnion* and *Alliance*: the author/scholar is not a
  viable named plaintiff; a fair-housing organization is support-only after *Alliance* and
  CoreLogic; the associational/P&A theory is derivative and inferior to naming the individual.
- **Authorities to verify independently.** The CoreLogic reporter citation should be confirmed
  against the published F.4th reporter. The associational-standing framework (*Hunt v. Washington
  State Apple Advertising Comm'n*, 432 U.S. 333 (1977)) and the P&A statutory predicate (DD Act;
  PAIMI Act; 29 U.S.C. § 794e) support only the subordinate organizational layer and should be
  independently verified before any reliance; the P&A operational-impairment sub-theory should not
  be built upon until they are. The *Lujan* footnote 7 pincite and quotation should likewise be
  independently confirmed.
- **Separate deferential-merits risk.** Even with a well-chosen plaintiff and clean finality, the
  petition can lose on the merits under deferential arbitrary-and-capricious review, and a
  published opinion sustaining HUD's non-engagement would be adverse precedent. The analysis
  supports litigating only a non-reasoned or record-ignoring denial, where remand under
  *Massachusetts v. EPA* is most available; a competent reasoned denial may be better left
  unlitigated, preserving the record and oversight value. This risk is a matter for independent
  legal judgment.
- **Zone of interests and Pennhurst.** Whether a disabled applicant falls within the zone of
  interests of § 3614a and § 3608(e)(6), and how that interacts with the *Pennhurst* clear-statement
  question, is noted but not resolved here.
