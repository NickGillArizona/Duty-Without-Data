# HUD's Existing Fragmented Disability-Data Architecture

HUD already operates program-specific and consent-decree-driven disability-data systems — Section 811 PRA with cross-agency individual-level tracking, the Los Angeles VCA's accessible-unit registry, and several program-level reporting architectures — but has never linked them into a coordinated federal system. The research below documents each fragment. HUD has the legal authority, technical capacity, and inter-agency cooperation; what is missing is the generalization.

**Companion to:** Nicholas Gill, *Duty Without Data: Disability Fair Housing and the Record-Dependent Right*, supplementary findings § 13.

**Sections:**

1. Section 811 PRA System of Records Notice (SORN) — cross-agency disability-housing data integration (82 Fed. Reg. 48,235)
2. HUD Voluntary Compliance Agreements and consent decrees — recipient-level disability-data architectures
3. HUD program-specific data architectures — Section 202 PRAC, Section 811 PRAC/PRA, HOPWA, LIHTC, MTW
4. REAC/NSPIRE physical inspection infrastructure — 34,649 properties with no accessibility output fields

## 1. Section 811 PRA SORN — Cross-Agency Disability-Housing Data Integration

**Primary source:** 82 FR 48,237 (Oct. 17, 2017) — "Privacy Act of 1974; System of Records: Section 811 Project Rental Assistance Evaluation-Phase II" (HUD PD&R).
**Document URL:** https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2017/10/17/2017-22474/

## Headline finding

**HUD itself has already built — and publicly noticed under the Privacy Act — exactly the cross-agency disability-housing data architecture the Note prescribes.** The Section 811 PRA Evaluation-Phase II SORN integrates HUD program data + Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Medicare/Medicaid data + six state Medicaid agencies' data + program-specific documents + neighborhood administrative data — all linked at individual disabled-resident level under a Privacy-Act-compliant routine-use framework.

**The Note's prescription is not novel design. It is generalization of the architecture HUD already operates for one program.**

## What the SORN actually integrates (verbatim from FR notice)

> "Primary data collection will include interviews with grantees and program partners and stakeholders and surveys of Section 811 Project Rental Assistance and Project Rental Assistance Contract residents. Secondary (existing) datasets will include HUD administrative data, Medicare and Medicaid data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), state Medicaid data from six state Medicaid agencies, Project Rental Assistance and Project Rental Assistance Contract program documents, and neighborhood administrative data."

This is, operationally, **the cross-program disability-housing data architecture the Note prescribes** — except limited to one program (Section 811 PRA) and one purpose (evaluation rather than ongoing administration).

## Operational components the SORN demonstrates HUD has

### 1. SORN compliance infrastructure
HUD has the SORN-drafting, PIA, FOIA-exemption-analysis, and Federal Register publication capacity to build new disability-data SORNs. The Section 811 PRA SORN is a four-page Federal Register notice published in 2017; the architecture has not been ad-hoc since.

### 2. Cross-agency MOU infrastructure with CMS
HUD already operates a CMS data-sharing MOU for the Section 811 PRA Evaluation. CMS Medicare/Medicaid data flows to HUD PD&R for evaluation purposes. **The interagency-data-sharing infrastructure exists.** CMS itself operates HCBS waiver disability-housing data architecture under § 1915(c). The HUD-CMS data-sharing relationship is established.

### 3. Cross-state data-integration capacity
The SORN integrates state Medicaid data from **six state Medicaid agencies**. HUD has the legal/operational infrastructure to negotiate state-by-state data-sharing agreements for disability-housing-data purposes. The architecture is replicable for additional states and for the broader prescription.

### 4. Resident-level disability data linked to program participation
The SORN explicitly tracks "extremely low-income nonelderly adults with disabilities" (per Section 811 PRA eligibility) at individual-resident level, linked to Medicare/Medicaid disability data, linked to program-administrative data. **This is the resident-level disability-data architecture the Note critiques HUD for lacking — and the Section 811 PRA SORN demonstrates HUD has built it for one program.**

### 5. Cross-program data integration
The Section 811 PRA SORN combines:
- HUD program-administrative data (grantee + tenant)
- CMS Medicare/Medicaid (federal disability + healthcare)
- State Medicaid data (six states)
- PRA / PRAC contract documents
- Neighborhood administrative data (likely Census-derived)

This is a five-source integration. The Note's prescription is similar-scope cross-source integration applied to mainstream HUD programs.

## Comparison: what the SORN does NOT yet do

The Section 811 PRA Evaluation SORN is limited in three ways relative to the Note's prescription:

1. **Limited to one program (Section 811 PRA), not cross-HUD-program**
2. **Limited to one purpose (evaluation), not ongoing administration**
3. **Limited to six states, not nationwide**

Each limitation is the design-choice issue the Note's prescription would address. **The technical, legal, and operational architecture is already in place; HUD's choice has been to apply it narrowly.**

## Why this is the most powerful "HUD already does this" argument

Other findings demonstrate HUD operates pieces of the prescribed architecture:
- Section 811 PRA + HOPWA: program-categorical disability data
- LA VCA + others: recipient-level RA-tracking architecture
- HUD-50058 / 50059: tenant-level disability fields collected
- USDA RHS MFIS: peer-federal-housing-agency disability-disaggregated data

But the **Section 811 PRA Evaluation-Phase II SORN is uniquely powerful** because it demonstrates the *integrated architecture* — cross-agency, cross-state, individual-disabled-resident-level, Privacy-Act-compliant, with active CMS data-sharing — already built and operating inside HUD. HUD has not just operated piece-by-piece; HUD has built the integrated architecture for one program. The Note's prescription is generalization.

## Honest limitations

- The Section 811 PRA Evaluation SORN is **for evaluation purposes**, not ongoing administration. Distinguishing evaluation-purpose SORNs from administration-purpose SORNs is real; the Note should not overclaim that the SORN is itself the prescribed architecture.
- The SORN was published in 2017; current operational status of the data-sharing arrangements should be verified before publication. The 2025 30-day notice for Section 811 PRA (2025-16765) suggests ongoing program reporting, but the Evaluation-Phase II project may have concluded.
- The SORN's "six state Medicaid agencies" identifies a limited geographic scope. Generalization requires negotiating with all 50 state Medicaid agencies — operationally feasible but resource-intensive.
- Some Section 811 PRA Evaluation findings have been published; full evaluation outputs may have constraints on public availability.

## 2. HUD VCAs and Consent Decrees — Recipient-Level Disability-Data Architectures

**Question presented:** Does HUD already impose recipient-level disability-data-collection requirements through its enforcement instruments — VCAs, conciliation agreements, and consent decrees — such that a unified disability-data architecture exists in fragmented form and could be aggregated?
**Short answer:** Yes. The strongest single piece of evidence is HUD's 2019 ten-year Voluntary Compliance Agreement with the City of Los Angeles, which directly imposes a public, quarterly-updated accessible-housing-unit registry; mandatory occupancy, transfer, and waiting-list audits with named-applicant rosters of disability accommodation needs; quarterly (then semiannual) HUD reporting; and full HUD records-access. Comparable but smaller-scale disability-data-tracking obligations appear in the Bridgeport, Baltimore, and Atlanta consent/VCA instruments; in HUD's standard FY 2022 conciliation/VCA template (e.g., Antioch Villas), which routinely orders six-month "reasonable accommodations/modifications log" submissions; and in the December 2021 NYCHA-FHEO PACT Assurances Agreement, which requires per-Round Accessible Housing Unit Plans and post-construction Completion Certifications. HUD already operates the apparatus the Note argues for; what is missing is generalization, not capacity.

---

## 1. Public HUD Voluntary Compliance Agreements (VCAs) with PHAs and Other Recipients

HUD's Office of Fair Housing and Equal Opportunity (FHEO) executes Voluntary Compliance Agreements following Section 504 / Title II / Fair Housing Act compliance reviews under 24 C.F.R. § 8.57; their public versions are posted on hud.gov, archives.hud.gov, and (for older agreements) the recipient's own websites. The single most important VCA for this Note is the **City of Los Angeles VCA executed August 2, 2019** (10-year term; Lynn M. Grosso (FHEO) and Richard Llewellyn (City) as signatories). The full 110-page agreement is available at archives.hud.gov/news/2019/HUD-City-of-Los-Angeles-VCA.pdf and is incorporated into the City's Affordable and Accessible Housing Registry. It directly compels disability-data architecture at the recipient level.

**Specific data-collection provisions in the LA VCA:** Section III.J ("Accessible Housing Website") (¶ 40) requires the City, within 180 days, to operate a public, accessible website that "lists all of the accessible Housing Units in the Housing Developments Covered by this Agreement (including Accessible Housing Units and Housing Units that comply with the accessibility requirements of the Fair Housing Act by Development, bedroom size, accessibility features, rent information (for available Housing Units), and other Unit and Development amenities and features"; the site must include an outreach signup, an online application, and a waiting-list function "that will enable individuals with disabilities to file on-line applications to rent available Accessible Housing Units or be placed on waiting lists for Accessible Housing Units." Section III.K imposes three named audits: an **Occupancy Audit** (¶ 41) that lists every designated accessible unit by development, address, unit number, bedroom size, and accessibility type, and identifies whether the current occupant requires the features; a **Transfer Audit** (¶ 42) listing each resident requesting transfer to an accessible unit, including name, address, required bedroom size, and date of transfer request; and a **Waiting List Audit** (¶ 43) examining "applicants with mobility disabilities and applicants with hearing or vision disabilities who have requested a Housing Unit with accessibility features," with reportable fields including applicant name, application date, required bedroom size, verification of need, any reasonable accommodation offers, and any reasonable accommodations requested. Section III.M ("Reporting and Record Keeping Requirements") (¶ 53) requires the VCA Administrator to "submit to the Department quarterly reports during the first twelve (12) months of this Agreement," then semi-annual reports thereafter, providing "quantitative and qualitative information on the actions the City has taken," "a list of grievances or complaints that were received by the City through the Grievance System (including copies of any written grievances or complaints)," and "detailed information on any noncompliance." Paragraph 54 obligates the City to "maintain all records relating to the City's, Subrecipients' and Owners' compliance with Section 504 (see 24 C.F.R. § 8.55) and the terms of this Agreement and shall require Subrecipients, Owners, and Property Management Agents to maintain all records relating to compliance." Paragraph 66 makes "any data and reports" generated under the agreement subject to public-records disclosure.

**Other public VCAs.** HUD has executed comparable disability-focused agreements with the **Atlanta Housing Authority** (three-year VCA announced Dec. 2022, $2 million victims fund; resolves a Section 504 compliance-review finding involving inadequate handling of reasonable-accommodation requests; press release at archives.hud.gov/news/2022/pr22-239.cfm); the **Housing Authority of Maricopa County, Arizona** (2021; resolves failure to fulfill a reasonable-accommodation request; archives.hud.gov/news/2021/pr21-157.cfm); the **Housing Authority of the City of Dallas** (2022 VCA-CA, $500,000 to a disabled tenant denied a unit-relocation accommodation, with revised reasonable-accommodation policies; FHEO FY 2022 Annual Report); the **Cuyahoga Metropolitan Housing Authority** (2022 VCA on disability discrimination at a project-based voucher property); and the M.J. Consulting / **Rio Plata / Puerto Rico Housing Finance Authority VCA** (2021; HUD Case Nos. 02-20-0030-4 (Section 504) and 02-20-0016-D (ADA); archives.hud.gov/news/2021/21VCA-Rio-Plata-PRHFA-4-26-2021.pdf). Each agreement contains some recipient-level disability data obligation — at minimum, recordkeeping of reasonable-accommodation logs and reports to HUD on accommodation outcomes — though the LA VCA is by far the most ambitious.

**What this proves about HUD's operational capacity.** HUD already drafts, administers, and monitors recipient-level disability data systems containing the exact fields the Note's argument calls for: counts of accessible units by accessibility type (mobility vs. hearing/vision), applicant rosters disaggregated by accessibility need, accommodation request logs, transfer-request rosters, vacancy and occupancy mismatch data, and grievance counts. HUD has the staff, the legal authority (under 24 C.F.R. § 8.55 and § 8.57), the document templates, and the periodic reporting cadence (quarterly to semi-annual). What it does not do is aggregate this data across recipients into a unified architecture; the data exist as discrete spreadsheets in compliance files for each VCA respondent.

## 2. HUD-DOJ Conciliation Agreements Under FHA Enforcement

HUD's FY 2022 State of Fair Housing Annual Report documents that **disability is the leading basis of FHA complaints**: in FY 2022, of 8,520 total complaints filed with HUD and its FHAP partners, 5,069 (59.5%) alleged disability discrimination — a five-year top-basis pattern. HUD attempts conciliation in every FHA complaint (per 42 U.S.C. § 3610(b)). The FY 2022 Report (available at hud.gov/sites/dfiles/FHEO/documents/FHEO%20Annual%20Report%20FY%202022.pdf) catalogs the year's disability conciliation/VCA agreements — and many include explicit, recipient-level data tracking.

**The clearest recurring data-collection clause** appears in the **Shaw and Millens v. Antioch Villas, L.P. (GA)** conciliation agreement (conciliated Nov. 5, 2021): respondents agreed to "provide the department with a reasonable accommodations/modifications log every six months for the term of the agreement, and attend fair housing training." This is HUD's standard template language for disability conciliation cases involving accommodation mishandling — a six-month, recipient-to-HUD log of all accommodation/modification requests with their dispositions. The same FHEO report identifies the 2022 Crespo v. DHS/Park View Hotel conciliation (service-animal denial; revised reasonable-accommodation policies and $50,000 payment); the 2022 Cuyahoga / Medina Creative Housing agreements (CMHA must monitor subrecipients for fair-housing compliance across all PHA programs); and the JCL Management / Pilot Cove VCA (waitlist reconstruction with marketing and outreach reporting requirements; full document at hud.gov/sites/dfiles/FHEO/documents/Pilot%20Cove%20Executed%20VCA.pdf). FHEO reported six VCAs executed in FY 2022 — each binding a specific recipient to recurring HUD reporting.

**HUD-DOJ pipeline.** Where HUD's compliance-review or conciliation efforts fail, 24 C.F.R. § 8.57 authorizes referral to the DOJ Civil Rights Division's Housing and Civil Enforcement Section. The DOJ catalogs these cases at justice.gov/crt/housing-and-civil-enforcement-cases-documents-232. The interface is operational: the LA VCA itself was reached only after HUD referred to DOJ in 2017 and DOJ deferred jurisdiction back to HUD for a renewed VCA. The combined HUD-DOJ enforcement instrument therefore has both a periodic-conciliation-report mode (FHEO oversight) and a court-supervised-data mode (when DOJ files), and disability-data tracking lives in both.

**What this proves.** HUD's existing complaint-driven conciliation architecture, applied to its largest single basis-class (disability), already routinely produces recipient-level accommodation-tracking data — typically deposited as a six-month log to HUD case officers. The data architecture is in place at the case-by-case level; what is not in place is a unified intake, a common schema, or a public aggregation. Aggregation would require no new statutory authority; HUD already has 24 C.F.R. § 8.55 record-keeping authority and uses it.

## 3. Court-Monitored Consent Decrees in Section 504 Housing Cases

DOJ has secured several consent decrees against PHAs under Section 504 / Title II / FHA. Three are particularly load-bearing:

**United States v. Housing Authority of Baltimore City** (D. Md., consent decree Sept. 29, 2004; supplemental decree Oct. 29, 2015 at justice.gov/d9/habc_supp_settle_10-29-15_0.pdf). The decree required HABC, over six years, to make available 755-756 fully UFAS-accessible public-housing units, including 30 scattered-site units, and to create 1,850 new housing opportunities for non-elderly persons with disabilities. As required under the decree, HABC created an internal **504 Coordination Office** at 417 E. Fayette Street, Suite 1305, Baltimore, MD 21202, responsible for monitoring HABC compliance with the Reasonable Accommodation Policy. The supplemental 2015 decree extended the production timeline through Dec. 31, 2016, with progress measured at the unit level (54 units remained at the time of the supplemental).

**United States v. Housing Authority of the City of Bridgeport, d/b/a Park City Communities** (D. Conn., consent order entered Nov. 5, 2019, after Nov. 15, 2017 DOJ complaint based on a HUD § 8.57 referral). The consent order requires HACB to "develop an inventory of accessible units for tenants with mobility, vision, and hearing-related disabilities" and to revise its reasonable-accommodation policies and processes. $1.5 million victim fund plus $25,000 civil penalty. The consent order is online at justice.gov/crt/case-document/consent-order-united-states-v-housing-authority-city-bridgeport-dba-park-city. The case is notable because it followed a *prior* HUD VCA that Bridgeport had not implemented — the consent order is HUD's enforcement backstop when a recipient fails its VCA, and it imposes the same data-collection architecture (accessible-unit inventory, accommodation tracking) by court decree rather than voluntary instrument.

**NYCHA / FHEO Section 504 PACT Assurances Agreement** (December 2021; covers Section 504 and ADA Title II compliance in PACT/RAD conversions; see nyc.gov/assets/nycha/downloads/pdf/r-pactnychafheo.pdf). The agreement requires no less than **7% Mobility units and 4% Hearing/Vision units** in PACT projects and provides that an "Accessible Housing Unit Plan addresses factors such as geographic distribution, dispersal within projects, unit size, unit occupancy criteria (preferences, elderly/family, etc.), and access on accessible routes in the aggregate for each proposed PACT/RAD Round." A NYCHA Accessibility Consultant reviews and certifies each PACT round and issues a post-construction Completion Certification. Although this is not a court-monitored consent decree in the formal sense, it operates with similar binding effect on a HUD recipient and is enforced by HUD (FHEO) and the independent monitor structure created by the 2019 NYCHA-HUD agreement. (Note: the original 1984/1995 *Williams v. NYCHA* consent decree is unrelated — it governs Section 8 termination procedures, not Section 504 accessibility.)

**Other related DOJ Section 504 enforcement** includes United States v. Park City Communities (CT) supra; the Cabrini-Green Consent Decree in Chicago (incorporating Section 504's 5%/2% scoping requirements as applied to Cabrini-Green redevelopment); and the now-active dispute around Access Living v. City of Chicago, in which DOJ filed a 2024 statement of interest alleging Chicago has failed Section 504 obligations across more than 500 affordable-housing complexes — pointing toward a potential additional decree of comparable scope to LA.

**What this proves.** Federal courts have, for over twenty years, ordered PHAs to build, maintain, and report on accessible-unit inventories; to designate Section 504 coordinators with named accountability; and to track accommodations and unit assignments by disability characteristics. HUD and DOJ have routinized this practice across at least Baltimore, Bridgeport, Los Angeles, Atlanta, Maricopa, Cuyahoga, Dallas, NYCHA, and the Rio Plata / PRHFA respondent. Each instrument generates a discrete, recipient-specific dataset of the type the Note argues HUD should be capable of unifying.

## Synthesis

The unified-disability-data-architecture that the Note advocates **already exists in fragmented form within HUD's enforcement portfolio**, distributed across at least three layers: (1) public Section 504 VCAs that impose registry-grade obligations on covered recipients (LA being the most expansive — public website, audits, named-applicant rosters, quarterly-then-semiannual HUD reporting under ¶¶ 40-43, 53-54); (2) routine FHA conciliation agreements where reasonable-accommodation logs flow to HUD on six-month cycles (Antioch Villas template); and (3) DOJ consent decrees that judicialize the same architecture when VCAs fail (Baltimore's 504 Coordination Office, Bridgeport's accessible-unit inventory). This Note's argument therefore does not ask HUD to create new operational capacity; it asks HUD to generalize existing capacity from individual respondents to all 3,300+ PHAs and other recipients. The 2023 GAO report ("HUD Should Collect and Maintain Reasonable Accommodation Request Data") confirms that HUD currently does not aggregate or systematically collect this data despite imposing it on individual respondents — a striking institutional asymmetry, and the strongest single fact in support of the Note's thesis. The architecture is already operational; it is simply not unified.

**Honest gaps in this research.** I could not directly read several primary documents because hud.gov and justice.gov enforce 403 blocks against the WebFetch tool, including the Bridgeport consent order, the HABC supplemental decree, the Atlanta VCA, and the Pilot Cove VCA — those summaries draw on press releases, the FHEO FY 2022 report, and authoritative third-party reportage rather than the operative documents. The exact fields and reporting cadences in the Bridgeport and HABC instruments may be tighter or looser than the LA VCA model presented above; the LA VCA has been read in the original. The author should pull the Bridgeport, HABC, and Atlanta documents directly through DOJ FOIA or a law-library service before quoting them in the Note. I have not been able to confirm whether HUD's "State of Fair Housing" annual reports include per-VCA data fields beyond the narrative case summaries presented in the FY 2022 edition; future drafts should examine the FY 2023-2025 annual reports for additional disability VCAs.

## 1. Section 202 PRAC — Supportive Housing for the Elderly

**Authority.** Section 202 of the Housing Act of 1959 (12 U.S.C. § 1701q); 24 CFR Part 891. Cranston-Gonzalez (P.L. 101-625) created the PRAC operating-subsidy variant beginning FY 1993.

**Data architecture.** Section 202 PRAC tenant data flow through the same TRACS/HUD-50059/HUD-52670 pipeline that the Note covers, under a distinct contract-type code. Program-level administrative system is REMS. Disability data on the 50059 reduces to "E" (elderly 62+) and "H" (handicap/disabled) special-status codes on head/co-head/spouse — these drive the elderly/disability deduction (HOTMA-raised $400 → $525 → $550 in 2026) and are the only structured disability fields routinely captured. A "NEAR" code marks near-elderly admissions. Pre-1992 grandfathered group homes for non-elderly disabled remain in the TRACS-202D dataset.

**Cadence.** Annual via HUD's *Picture of Subsidized Households* (PSH) for program code 8 (202/PRAC). A 2015 HUD PD&R PIC/TRACS extract reported ~302,000 households across the Section 202 universe, ~263,000 elderly. No standing Section 202-specific tenant disability report exists.

**Disability integration.** Structurally undertheorized: program defined by elderly status, "H" coding captured but never surfaced as a public category — despite aging-in-place producing a large and growing in-unit disabled population.

**Model potential.** **Weak proof-of-concept.** Same E/H codes already documented in the Note. Useful primarily to illustrate *aging-in-place data invisibility*: the architecture does not track elderly→elderly-and-disabled transitions.

## 2. Section 811 PRAC and Section 811 PRA — Supportive Housing for Persons with Disabilities

**Authority.** Section 811 of Cranston-Gonzalez (42 U.S.C. § 8013); 24 CFR Part 891. The 2010 Frank Melville Act (P.L. 111-374) created the 811 PRA variant administered by state HFAs in partnership with state Medicaid agencies, alongside the legacy 811 PRAC capital-grant model.

**Data architecture — the most disability-saturated record system HUD operates.** 811 PRAC tenant records flow through TRACS via HUD-50059 (same E/H special-status coding) but with categorical eligibility conditioned on disability. 811 PRA additionally requires a state Medicaid/HHS agency partnership: each state agency identifies, refers, and tracks extremely-low-income non-elderly (18–61) disabled adults eligible for 811 PRA units. Eligibility verification requires Medicaid documentation. Quarterly and annual reports from state housing agencies flow to HUD on unit utilization, tenant outcomes, and Medicaid-services linkage.

For evaluation, HUD's PD&R built a dedicated **System of Records — "Section 811 PRA Evaluation-Phase II"** (Privacy Act SORN, 82 Fed. Reg. 48235, Oct. 17, 2017) linking HUD administrative data, CMS Medicare/Medicaid data, state Medicaid data from six states, program documents, neighborhood administrative data, and a survey of ~400 PRA/PRAC residents. **This is HUD's existing disability-categorical data architecture, already operating, already linked to Medicaid.**

**Cadence.** Annual via PSH (program code 9). HUD published the Process Evaluation (2018), Phase II Final Report (2020), six case studies, and Phase II Executive Summary on huduser.gov. No standing annual public dataset on 811 tenant outcomes; reporting episodic via PD&R commissioned studies.

**Disability integration.** Total. 97.9% disability prevalence is structural; eligibility conditioned on it; Medicaid linkage built in. The architecture treats disability as the targeting axis, not a side variable.

**Model potential.** **Strongest proof-of-concept**, alongside HOPWA. Section 811 PRA demonstrates HUD can: (i) define a tenant-level disability category with a verification rule; (ii) operate state HFA–Medicaid agency referral pipelines; (iii) link CMS data to TRACS via a publicly noticed Privacy Act SORN; (iv) sustain quarterly reporting on disability targeting; (v) commission longitudinal evaluations on the linked architecture. The Note's argument: *HUD already runs this for one program — Part 121 generalizes what 811 already does.*

## 3. HOPWA — Housing Opportunities for Persons With AIDS

**Authority.** AIDS Housing Opportunity Act, 42 U.S.C. § 12901 et seq. (Title VIII of Cranston-Gonzalez); 24 CFR Part 574. The only federal program exclusively dedicated to housing low-income people living with HIV/AIDS.

**Data architecture — densest HUD disability data architecture by element count.** Two parallel systems:
1. **HUD-40110-D Consolidated APR/CAPER** (formula grantees) — annual report consolidated 2024 into a single Excel workbook covering grant activities, sponsors, housing sites, units, households, beneficiaries with racial/ethnic data.
2. **HMIS data standards** — HOPWA-funded projects submit to CoC HMIS using Universal + Program-Specific Data Elements:
   - **Universal:** 3.08 Disabling Condition; 3.04 Race and Ethnicity
   - **Program-Specific:** 4.05 Physical Disability; 4.06 Developmental Disability; 4.08 HIV/AIDS Status; 4.09 Mental Health; 4.10 Substance Use Disorder; 4.11 Chronic Health Condition
   - **HOPWA-only:** W1 Services; W2 Financial Assistance; W3 Medical Assistance; W4 T-cell (CD4) and Viral Load; W5 Housing Assessment at Exit; W6 Anti-Retroviral

This is structurally **a disability-categorical architecture with five sub-disability fields plus clinical (T-cell, viral load) data.** HMIS local systems must close-share HIV status only with case-management providers.

**Cadence.** Annual CAPER per grantee year; HUD publishes summary statistics annually on huduser.gov and hudexchange.info; formula allocations annually.

**Disability integration.** Multi-dimensional. HIV/AIDS itself is disability under *Bragdon v. Abbott*, 524 U.S. 624 (1998); architecture additionally captures physical, developmental, mental health, substance use, and chronic health as separate per-beneficiary fields.

**Model potential.** **Strongest proof-of-concept**, alongside 811. HOPWA proves HUD can: (i) require a disability sub-typology (multiple categories tracked separately); (ii) integrate clinical data with housing data; (iii) sustain HMIS submission with privacy protections; (iv) consolidate CAPER reporting into a public-facing form; (v) publish aggregated outcomes annually. *If HUD has sustained HOPWA's disability sub-typology for 30+ years, the claim that Part 121 architecture is infeasible across programs collapses.*

## 4. VAWA Housing Protections — Data Collection Architecture

**Authority.** VAWA 1994 as reauthorized 2005, 2013, 2022 (P.L. 117-103); 34 U.S.C. §§ 12491–12492 (sections 41411–41412). VAWA 2022 expanded "covered housing program" to virtually every HUD rental subsidy program plus a catch-all. Section 41412 added agency compliance review with mandatory public disclosure.

**Data architecture.** Five model forms under OMB Control No. 2577-0286:
- HUD-5380 — Notice of Occupancy Rights
- HUD-5381 — Model Emergency Transfer Plan
- HUD-5382 — Certification of Domestic Violence
- HUD-5383 — Emergency Transfer Request
- **HUD-5384 — VAWA Emergency Transfer Data Collection Form** (only aggregate-reporting form; OMB approval through 1/31/2028)

HUD-5384 collects aggregate (not per-survivor) data to protect confidentiality: approved/denied transfer counts, processing timeframes, denial reasons. The 30-Day ICR Notice (89 FR 57903, July 16, 2024) added annual collections on whether PHAs/owners adopted transfer policies, waiting-list preferences, consortium coordination.

**Cadence.** Annual aggregate at the covered-housing-provider level; HUD compliance review reporting under VAWA 2022 § 602 to be incorporated into existing compliance review processes; agency-level assessments must be publicly disclosed.

**Disability integration.** **Functionally absent.** VAWA requires reasonable accommodations to enable equal access, but HUD-5384 does not segment emergency-transfer data by tenant disability status — neither approved nor denied transfers tracked by disability. Disability appears in the *rights notice* (5380) but not the *outcome reporting* (5384). HUD's own reporting cannot detect disparate processing-time, denial-rate, or transfer-availability outcomes for disabled survivors.

**Model potential.** **Useful counter-example.** HUD built a brand-new aggregate-reporting form (5384) post-statute — proving HUD designs new tenant-data instruments when statutorily required — but stripped disability segmentation. Evidence that HUD's data choices are discretionary, not technically constrained.

## 5. HOTMA — Housing Opportunity Through Modernization Act of 2016

**Authority.** P.L. 114-201 (July 29, 2016) amending U.S. Housing Act of 1937 §§ 3, 8, 16. Final rule 88 FR 9600 (Feb. 14, 2023, effective Jan. 1, 2024); PIH 2023-27 / H 2023-10 implementing notice (Sept. 29, 2023).

**Data architecture changes affecting disability data.** Principal effects:
- **§ 102 income reviews:** new triennial/annual reexamination rules; medical/disability assistance income uses $0 floor with 10%-of-annual-income disregard, requiring new HUD-50058/HUD-50059 fields capturing the disregard.
- **§ 104 asset rules:** necessary personal-property assets (including assistive technology) excluded — but only if owners code them as such.
- **Elderly/disabled deduction:** $400 → $525 (1/1/2024) → $550 (2026); now inflation-indexed.
- **Hardship exemptions:** disability-related hardship exemptions require new fields on tenant certifications.

The rule explicitly requires updates to TRACS, the Housing Information Portal (HIP), and IMS-PIC. Full compliance extended to January 1, 2027. **HOTMA is the most recent statutory occasion when Congress materially restructured what HUD-50058/50059 must capture about disability — and HUD did update the forms.**

**Cadence.** No new HOTMA-specific public report; changes feed into existing PSH/PIC/TRACS streams.

**Disability integration.** Substantial increase in *number* of disability-impacted fields (asset exclusion coding, hardship flags, indexed deductions, disregard calculation), but no new tenant-level disability *categorization* — still a binary disability flag for head/spouse/co-head.

**Model potential.** **Moderate proof-of-concept.** Recent operational capability to push significant new disability-touching fields through TRACS, HIP, IMS-PIC simultaneously plus update model forms (50058 revised; 50059 in queue). Directly rebuts any claim that adding fields to existing certification forms is technically prohibitive.

## 6. Moving to Work (MTW) — PHA Disability Data Flexibility

**Authority.** Section 204 of the FY 1996 VA-HUD Appropriations Act (P.L. 104-134) created the original MTW Demonstration; Consolidated Appropriations Act 2016 (P.L. 114-113 § 239) authorized the MTW Expansion (100 new PHAs across cohorts). PIH 2021-03 covers Cohort 4. OMB Control No. 2577-0083.

**Data architecture.** MTW PHAs may submit the **abbreviated HUD-50058 MTW Family Report** in place of the standard 50058:
- Demographic, family profile, income, housing data still collected
- **Rent calculation data omitted** — MTW PHAs use alternative rent rules
- Disability and elderly fields **retained** (statutory deduction continues; work-requirement waivers may not apply to elderly/disabled families)
- MTW Expansion Family Report similarly abbreviated

Reporting cadence is monthly to the IMS-PIC MTW module. The MTW Supplement (annual activity report) and MTW Annual Plan flow on a separate annual cycle (60-Day ICR Notice 2025-16971, Sept. 4, 2025).

**Cadence.** MTW Plans/Supplements posted publicly per PHA. PD&R publishes episodic aggregate research: *Evaluation of MTW Flexibility Cohort: Year 3 Report* (April 2026) — 279 approved waivers, 71% implemented by end of 2023.

**Disability integration.** MTW PHAs collect *less* tenant-level data because the rent-calculation block is omitted and MTW PHAs may waive admission/recertification rules. Disability and elderly fields retained because federal law requires work-requirement exclusions and equal access for disabled/elderly families.

**Model potential.** **Counter-example.** Data-collection rules track HUD's policy choices, not fixed technical constraints — when HUD wants less data it builds an abbreviated form; when HUD wants activity-level reporting on dozens of waivers across PHAs, it builds a fillable supplement and a public database. Disability data could be expanded through the same mechanism.

## 7. Section 8 PBV (Project-Based Voucher) and NAHASDA — Specific Subset Architectures

### 7a. Section 8 Project-Based Voucher (PBV)

**Authority.** 42 U.S.C. § 1437f(o)(13); 24 CFR Part 983.

**Data architecture.** PBV tenant data flow through the same HUD-50058 reported via IMS-PIC. PBV-specific additions: HUD-52578-B Statement of Family Responsibility and the **PBV Online Form** (60-day ICR notice 84 FR 71046, Dec. 26, 2019). PBV requires PHAs to track:
- Population-served categories per project: General, Homeless, Veterans, Families Eligible for Supportive Services, Families Receiving Supportive Services, **Elderly Family**, **Disabled Family**
- Accessible units per project (24 CFR Part 8 / Section 504)
- Reasonable accommodations and accessible-unit availability under 24 CFR 8.27
- HQS lines 5h and 5i are not applicable to PBV (HCV-only)

**Cadence.** Annual via PSH (PBV captured separately from tenant-based HCV) plus SEMAP-equivalent aggregate reporting.

**Disability integration.** Both tenant-level (HUD-50058 disability flag) and project-level (population-served category, accessible-unit count under 24 CFR 8.27). The *project*-level disability data architecture exists — accessible units are inventoried per project — but national aggregation is fragmented.

**Model potential.** **Moderate proof-of-concept.** HUD already collects disability-categorical project-level data; the gap is national aggregation and publication, not collection.

### 7b. NAHASDA / IHBG (Native American Housing)

**Authority.** NAHASDA 1996 (P.L. 104-330), 25 U.S.C. § 4101 et seq.; 24 CFR Part 1000.

**Data architecture.** IHBG recipients submit:
- **Indian Housing Plan (IHP)** — 75 days before program year start
- **Annual Performance Report (APR)** — Form HUD-52737, 90 days after program year end (OMB 2577-0218)
- Submitted electronically via GEMS

**Critical difference from other HUD programs:** Per 24 CFR Part 1000 Subpart F, **the Federal Privacy Act does not apply to NAHASDA recipient records**, and FOIA does not apply to recipient records. Tribal self-determination governs records access. HUD does not maintain a tenant-level federal database of IHBG-assisted households comparable to PIC/TRACS. The IHBG formula uses Census-derived AIAN need variables (income bands, overcrowding, cost burden) and Formula Current Assisted Stock (FCAS); no tribe-specific tenant disability variable enters the formula.

HUD-52737 captures planned/actual housing activities by category. 24 CFR § 1000.10 defines "elderly family" and "disabled family" — but tenant-level disability data is collected (if at all) at the tribal/TDHE level, not centralized at HUD.

**Cadence.** Annual Report to Congress on NAHASDA (FY 2024 published); aggregate program-level reporting only.

**Disability integration.** Definitionally present in regulatory family categories; **functionally absent** at federal data architecture level because the Privacy Act exemption and self-determination structure preclude HUD-level tenant-level collection. GAO-15-353R flagged IHBG data limitations.

**Model potential.** **Inverse proof-of-concept.** NAHASDA's structure intentionally limits HUD-level data centralization to protect tribal sovereignty — Part 121 must carve out NAHASDA. But NAHASDA also shows HUD *can* operate a major housing program with no central tenant database, meaning the existence of central databases for non-Tribal programs is a deliberate design choice.

## Synthesis: Which Programs Are the Strongest Proof of Concept for Part 121

Ranking the seven programs as proof-of-concept arguments that HUD already has the operational capacity for a unified Part 121 disability-data architecture:

1. **Section 811 PRA (and PRAC)** — *Strongest*. HUD already operates a categorical disability-data architecture, links it to CMS Medicaid data through a publicly noticed Privacy Act SORN (82 FR 48235), runs state-Medicaid agency intake/referral pipelines, and sustains quarterly state-level reporting. A working model of every operational element Part 121 would need: categorical eligibility, cross-agency data linkage, state intermediary reporting, longitudinal evaluation. *Generalizing 811's data design is an extension, not an invention.*

2. **HOPWA** — *Strongest alternative model*. 30+ years of operational capacity for a disability sub-typology (not a binary flag): physical, developmental, mental health, substance use, chronic condition — plus clinical integration (T-cell, viral load, anti-retroviral). HMIS standards + consolidated APR/CAPER prove HUD can require, collect, aggregate, and publish disability-segmented data with privacy protections. HUD already maintains the *kind of sub-typology* disability-rights scholarship argues is necessary — just not generalized.

3. **HOTMA** — *Strongest contemporary precedent*. The 2016 statute and 2023 final rule prove recent bandwidth to push new disability-touching fields through TRACS, HIP, and IMS-PIC simultaneously plus update model forms. Forecloses any "we can't change the systems" defense.

4. **Section 8 PBV** — *Moderate*. HUD already collects disability-categorical project-level data (population-served categories, accessible-unit counts under 24 CFR 8.27); gap is national aggregation, not collection.

5. **MTW** — *Counter-example*. HUD designs *more* and *less* tenant data per policy choice — no fixed technical limit.

6. **VAWA HUD-5384** — *Counter-example*. HUD recently designed a brand-new aggregate-reporting form post-statute but stripped disability segmentation — disability omission is discretionary, not necessary.

7. **Section 202 PRAC** — *Weak; useful for aging-in-place gap*. Same TRACS architecture as 811; illustrates aging-in-place data invisibility.

8. **NAHASDA** — *Inverse proof-of-concept; structural carve-out*. Tribal sovereignty means Part 121 must carve out NAHASDA, but NAHASDA also shows HUD *can* run a major program with no central tenant database — meaning the existence of central databases for non-Tribal programs is deliberate, not necessary.

**Key takeaway:** Section 811 PRA + HOPWA together establish that HUD has built the categorical disability-data architecture (with cross-agency linkage and disability sub-typology) that the Note proposes to generalize. HOTMA establishes contemporary systems-update bandwidth. The remaining programs show HUD's *failure* to generalize is discretionary, not technical.

## Finding 1: REAC/NSPIRE Inspects 34,649 Properties Nationwide — With Zero Accessibility Items

### Hypothesis

The note's Tier 2 proposal argues that HUD should add a defined accessibility module to the NSPIRE inspection framework it already administers. The Note currently asserts that NSPIRE "measures physical condition—grab bars, handrails, unit dimensions—but does not inspect for the seven design-and-construction requirements of section 3604(f)(3)(C)." That claim rests on the NSPIRE Terms and Definitions documentation. If the published inspection score data confirms this gap at scale — showing that tens of thousands of properties are inspected without any accessibility output — the Tier 2 argument moves from aspirational to empirically grounded.

### Method

Analyzed two HUD datasets obtained from Data.gov:
- `public_housing_physical_inspection_scores.xlsx` (1.0 MB, 6,190 records)
- `multifamily_physical_inspection_scores.xlsx` (4.5 MB, 28,459 records)

For each dataset: examined the complete schema (all column names and data types), counted unique properties, identified the date range of inspections, catalogued all inspection-output fields, and performed keyword searches across every string column for terms related to accessibility, disability, ADA, Section 504, design-and-construction, fair housing, and barriers.

### Key Findings

#### 1. Scale of the existing inspection infrastructure

| Metric | Public Housing | Multifamily | Combined |
|---|---|---|---|
| Properties inspected | 6,190 | 28,459 | **34,649** |
| Date range | Jun 2013 -- Jul 2025 | Feb 2013 -- Aug 2025 | ~12 years |
| States/territories covered | 54 | 55 | Nationwide |
| Unique cities | 2,581 | 5,411 | — |
| NSPIRE protocol share | 55.1% | 55.1% | 55.1% |
| UPCS (legacy) protocol share | 44.9% | 44.9% | 44.9% |

#### 2. What the inspection data contains

Both datasets share a nearly identical 18-20 column schema consisting exclusively of:
- Property identification (ID, name, address)
- Geographic identifiers (city, county, CBSA, state, zip, lat/long)
- A single aggregate inspection result (INSPECTION_ID, INSPECTION_SCORE, INSPECTION_DATE, INSPECTION_PROTOCOL)
- Public housing file adds PHA_CODE and PHA_NAME

The only inspection output is a **single integer score (0-100)** per property per inspection cycle.

#### 3. What the inspection data does NOT contain

Neither dataset contains:
- Any sub-scores or inspection category breakdowns
- Any column referencing accessibility, disability, ADA, Section 504, design-and-construction, fair housing, or physical barriers
- Any indication of what specific deficiency items were assessed
- Any data on compliance with section 3604(f)(3)(C) requirements

A comprehensive keyword search across every string column in both files for "accessib," "disab," "ada," "section 504," "design and construction," "fair housing," and "barrier" returned only incidental matches in property names and street addresses (e.g., properties named "Accessible Apts. No 1" or addresses on "Ada Street").

#### 4. Inspection score distributions

| Statistic | Public Housing | Multifamily |
|---|---|---|
| Mean score | 83.1 | 89.4 |
| Median score | 88 | 92 |
| Std Dev | 15.4 | 11.0 |
| Range | 0–100 | 0–100 |
| Scoring 80+ | 69.9% | 86.6% |
| Scoring 90+ | 39.9% | 60.0% |

The high average scores (83-89) mean most properties pass physical-condition inspections — but that "passing" says nothing about accessibility compliance.

### Implications for the Note

This is direct empirical support for the Tier 2 "marginal step" argument. The note can now make three claims with data behind them:

1. **Scale:** HUD already inspects over 34,000 assisted properties nationwide through REAC/NSPIRE. The inspection infrastructure exists and operates at the scale the Tier 2 proposal requires.

2. **Gap:** That infrastructure produces zero data on whether inspected properties comply with federal accessibility mandates. A property can score 100 on its NSPIRE inspection while violating every section 3604(f)(3)(C) design-and-construction requirement.

3. **Marginal step:** Adding a defined accessibility module to NSPIRE is not building a new inspectorate. It is adding inspection items to an existing framework that already sends inspectors to these properties, already scores deficiency criteria, and already imposes correction timeframes. The marginal cost is inspector training and checklist development, not institutional creation.

This strengthens Part III.C (where Tier 2 is described), Part IV.D (where the administrative-feasibility objection is answered), and the general credibility of the proposal. The 34,649-property figure is also relevant to the geographic reach argument: unlike entitlement-community-based planning, NSPIRE inspections operate wherever HUD-assisted properties exist, including non-entitlement communities.

## Finding 2: The 2019 HUD Charge Rate Is Verified at 0.09%

### Hypothesis

The pipeline memo cited a figure of 7 HUD reasonable-cause charges from 7,801 FHEO filed cases in 2019 (a 0.09% charge rate), sourced from Data.gov. The memo flagged this as an "unusually low outlier that should be verified." Separately, the NFHA 2025 report shows 37 charges for 2019. The discrepancy needed resolution before the number could be used.

### Method

Opened `fha-cases-by-year.xls` (Data.gov, HUD FHEO data, 2000-2019) and extracted the 2019 national totals. Cross-referenced against `fheo-filed-cases.xlsx` (case-level microdata) for the 2019 filing count.

### Key Findings

| Source | Filed Cases (2019) | Charges (2019) | Charge Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| HUD Data.gov (`fha-cases-by-year.xls`) | 7,801 | **7** | **0.09%** |
| NFHA 2025 Report | — | **37** | — |
| Difference | — | 30 | — |

The 7-charge figure is verified directly from HUD's published data. The 7 charges came from 6 states: Colorado (1), Georgia (1), Massachusetts (1), Oregon (1), Pennsylvania (2), Rhode Island (1).

The discrepancy with the NFHA figure (37) is definitional: HUD's Data.gov dataset reports **HUD-direct reasonable-cause charges only** — cases where HUD itself (through FHEO) found reasonable cause and issued a formal charge. The NFHA figure includes charges issued by **FHAP agencies** (state and local Fair Housing Assistance Program partners that handle complaints under substantially equivalent state/local laws). The difference (30) represents FHAP-agency cause determinations.

Both numbers are accurate; they measure different things. The HUD-direct figure (7 from 7,801 = 0.09%) specifically captures the federal agency's own enforcement output. The NFHA figure (37) captures the combined federal-plus-state enforcement output.

Additional context from `fha-cases-by-year.xls` for 2019:
- No reasonable cause: 3,736 (47.9% of filed)
- Conciliations/Settlements: 1,774 (22.7%)
- Administrative closures: 706 (9.0%)
- Open cases: 665 (8.5%)
- Charges: 7 (0.09%)

### Implications for the Note

The 0.09% HUD-direct charge rate in 2019 is a verified, defensible number that dramatically illustrates the administrative enforcement nadir. It is now safe to use.

Two possible uses:
1. **In the pipeline paragraph (II.C):** The current text says "Only approximately 1.5% of filed disability complaints result in a charge or cause finding." That 1.5% is a 2024 estimate combining HUD and FHAP charges. Adding: "HUD's own charge rate reached a nadir of 0.09% in 2019 — seven charges from 7,801 filed cases — before the post-2024 contraction the note documents" would show that the pipeline was already broken before the current crisis.

2. **In a footnote:** If the body text should remain focused on the 2024 pipeline, the 2019 figure belongs in a footnote supporting the claim that the enforcement pipeline's deterioration predates *Loper Bright* and the 2024 political changes.

**Caveat for citation:** If used, the note should specify "HUD-direct reasonable-cause charges" to distinguish from the combined HUD + FHAP figure. The citation is: Data.gov, FHEO Filed Title VIII Cases by Year and State (2000-2019), national total row, 2019.

## Finding 3: Disability's Share of FHEO Filings Nearly Doubled Over Two Decades

### Data

From `fha-cases-by-year.xls` and `pipeline_results.json` (FHEO historical series):

| Year | FHEO Filed Cases | Disability-Basis Cases | Disability Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2000 | 7,116 | 2,454 | 34.5% |
| 2005 | 9,455 | 3,967 | 42.0% |
| 2010 | 10,326 | 4,878 | 47.2% |
| 2015 | 8,212 | 4,572 | 55.7% |
| 2019 | 7,801 | 4,848 | **62.1%** |
| 2024 (NFHA) | — | 17,645 | **54.6%** |

Disability's share of FHEO-filed cases rose from 34.5% in 2000 to 62.1% in 2019 — a near-doubling. The 2024 NFHA figure (54.6%) uses a different denominator that includes FHO, HUD, FHAP, and DOJ complaints, making it not directly comparable to the FHEO-only series.

### Implications for the Note

This trend strengthens Part II.A's claim that disability is "AFFH's central blind spot." The note already cites the 54.6% figure from the 2024 NFHA report. Adding the 20-year trajectory in a footnote would show that the blind spot has been growing for two decades: the protected class generating the largest and fastest-growing share of federal fair housing complaints received the weakest AFFH operationalization.

Best placement: as a footnote addition to the existing text in the introduction or Part II.A that says "Disability generates 54.6 percent of all fair-housing complaints."

## Data Quality Notes

1. **Inspection score data** is published by HUD on Data.gov and reflects actual REAC/NSPIRE inspection results. The absence of accessibility items from the published data does not definitively prove that inspectors never observe accessibility features — it proves that the published inspection framework does not systematically assess or report on them. The note's NSPIRE Terms and Definitions citation ([^191]) confirms that the inspection protocol itself does not include section 3604(f)(3)(C) items; the inspection score data confirms this at the level of actual published outputs.

2. **The 2019 charge figure** (7) comes from HUD's own published aggregate data. It is an unusually low year — the 2014-2020 average is 30.3 HUD-direct charges per year. 2019 may reflect internal processing delays, staffing, or case-management factors rather than a steady-state enforcement capacity.

3. **The FHEO disability share series** uses the disability-basis column in `fha-cases-by-year.xls`, which counts cases where disability was listed as a basis of discrimination. Cases may list multiple bases; the disability count is not mutually exclusive with other protected-class counts.

4. **The 2024 NFHA disability share** (54.6%) uses a different denominator (all complaints across FHOs, HUD, FHAP, and DOJ) and is not directly comparable to the FHEO-only series. The apparent decline from 62.1% (2019 FHEO-only) to 54.6% (2024 all-source) likely reflects the inclusion of FHO-processed complaints, which may have a different protected-class distribution than HUD/FHAP filings.
