# Duty Without Data: Disability Fair Housing and the Record-Dependent Right

Nicholas Gill[^star][^1]

## ABSTRACT

Disability fair-housing rights under 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3), § 504, and 24 C.F.R. Part 8 are *record-dependent*: ordinary administration and proof of their operative elements — notice, nexus, timing, denial, covered status, accessible-unit count, and allocation — turn on records of regulated-party conduct. Those records are not absent; they are fragmented: HUD's own instruments each create pieces of the record chain, but no identified instrument reliably classifies, preserves, links, transmits, or makes accessible to claimants the transaction-level records the enacted duties presuppose. The summer of 2026 supplied a stress test: transaction records, asset records, and aggregate reports must each stand on their own statutory footing.

An original corpus of 1,900 screened federal opinion and order records documents the enforcement setting. Across 606 identified decided-opinion cases, each counted once, eighteen qualifying plaintiff-side judgments issued over four and a half years: nine final contested judgments, two defaults, and seven liability determinations with the remedy reserved. The windowed series supports neither a decline nor an increase. In every one, counsel had appeared by the qualifying disposition; none arose among the 400 identified pro se cases, while the case-level pro se share rose from roughly 60% to 76%. These figures describe the captured opinion-bearing pipeline, not claim success, settlement outcomes, or the causal effect of counsel or records.

The remedy operationalizes a bounded form of record reciprocity through a modular § 553(e) petition for a defined minimum-record rule grounded in § 3614a, § 3608(e)(6), § 3608(f), § 504, and Part 8: a dual-source record of each transaction the provider classifies as disability-related or the requester so designates, with a contemporaneous accessible copy to the requester; accessible-unit inventories; the aggregate oversight fields § 3608(e)(6) separately commands; and — regulator-only and most readily severed — restricted unit-to-household matching records. Each module is severable, implementation vehicles left to HUD, under one limiting principle: *verification without substantive expansion* — fields reasonably necessary to administer already-enacted duties, altering no liability standard and conditioning no right on a prescribed form. The petition's primary value is administrative — a public accounting of what HUD already collects and why the gaps persist; judicial review of a denial is a contingent, module-specific, exceptionally deferential backstop.

## INTRODUCTION

In May 2010, the Mobile Housing Board issued Donavette Ely a four-bedroom voucher; the Board's own written explanation cited her son Devin's "present medical condition."[^2] Ely could not find an affordable four-bedroom unit; the Board granted one extension, denied more time, and later removed her from Section 8. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed: the Board "cannot be liable for refusing to grant a reasonable and necessary accommodation if the [Board] never knew the accommodation was in fact necessary," because Ely "never explained to the Board that she needed a voucher extension to compensate for her son's disability."[^3] *Ely* illustrates both the value and the limit of the record chain. The Board had created and preserved a written disability-related basis for the voucher; what failed was linkage — nothing carried that basis, in a form legible as disability-related, into the extension decision where it counted.[^4] The limit is equally instructive: no record system can substitute for a request that never communicates its disability connection, and no record rule would have determined liability; a linked record would simply have surfaced the Board's own earlier explanation inside an extension decision Part 8 already commits to the public housing authority (PHA) (Part II.E).

That failure now has a documented administrative-record sequence. Congress authorized HUD in 1988 to issue rules for the collection, maintenance, and analysis of appropriate data, and HUD's 24 C.F.R. Part 121 has identified handicap and family characteristics since 1989.[^5] Between 2022 and 2026, HUD proposed a Form HUD-27061 protected-class collection invoking Part 121, then — without explanation in the located record — narrowed it to the race-and-ethnicity collection the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) approved in 2023, and in June 2026 proposed to renew the narrowed form unchanged as the approval reached its June 30, 2026 expiration with no successor on record in the public Paperwork Reduction Act (PRA) file.[^6][^144] The claim is deliberately narrow — HUD collects race data in other systems and publishes participant-level disability counts — but the sequence converts Part 121's fragmented implementation into administrative retreat on the one instrument HUD itself had placed in the record.

The resulting problem is the space between substantive disability duties and the records needed to verify them.[^8] Roughly 1.80 million HUD-assisted households have a disabled head or spouse, and HUD's own instruments already create pieces of the relevant record.[^7] What no instrument supplies is the chain: records reliably created, classified as disability-related, preserved, linked across actors and decisions, accessible to the claimant, and aggregated for oversight. AFFH planning has proved politically unstable,[^9][^10] and Part II leaves private litigation unestablished as an adequate substitute. The Note's contribution is therefore narrower than a general demand for more data: when enacted duties turn on facts the regulated institution controls or shapes, the remedy begins with a minimally sufficient, claimant-accessible transaction record, supplemented by asset and oversight records on their own statutory footing after *Loper Bright*.[^11]

The summer of 2026 supplies a stress test, not the Note's foundation. In June 2026, the Office of Legal Counsel concluded that neither § 504 nor Title II imposed an integration mandate on states in their treatment of persons with mental disabilities — and that the HHS and DOJ regulations codifying that mandate exceed the agencies' statutory authority.[^173] On July 20, DOJ declared its *Olmstead* enforcement guidance "not enforceable."[^174] The next day, the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission approved a proposal to rescind the EEO-1 through EEO-6 reports and the report-tied recordkeeping, while expressly retaining the general record-preservation provisions whose text reaches requests for reasonable accommodation.[^175] This Note claims only the half each action supports: guidance can vanish overnight while the records the underlying duties presuppose remain the condition of administering whatever rule lawfully governs (DOJ); and codification alone is not durability — each field must rest on the best reading of a specific statutory command, the discipline Part III imposes (OLC). And the EEOC proposal draws, inside a single regulatory part, the Note's own distinction: a transaction record is not a recurring demographic report, and the two need not share a legal fate (Part III.E).

Part I defines the bounded category and maps the broken record chain; Part II locates private enforcement downstream from that chain; Part III translates the theory into field-specific authority and design; Part IV tests the petition as an implementation vehicle and contingent backstop.

# PART I: THE ANATOMY OF A RECORD-DEPENDENT RIGHT

A right is record-dependent when its ordinary administration or proof depends materially on transaction-, unit-, or project-specific facts that are not reliably created, classified, preserved, linked, made accessible to the claimant, or aggregated for oversight. Claims can fail even when the underlying facts favor the plaintiff, because the record chain breaks at one or more links. At the transaction level the chain has four links — creation; designation as disability-related, by provider classification or requester designation; preservation and linkage across responsible actors and successive decisions; and claimant access — running from the request through the response or, where none issues, the unresolved status. For unit- and project-level duties the parallel object is an asset record; a distinct oversight cycle — standardization, transmission, aggregation — operates on both; aggregation can reveal a pattern but proves no individual claim. One function is deliberately excluded: *legal translation* — converting preserved facts into the elements a complaint must allege — is a legal-services function no record system supplies; a stable record narrows the reconstruction problem on which translation operates, but does not perform it.

The category is bounded — all rights depend on evidence. Record-dependence does distinctive work only where (1) operative elements turn on transaction-specific facts; (2) the regulated party controls or shapes the contemporaneous account, with the underlying documentation distributed among claimant, physician, provider, PHA, owner, and HUD; (3) no mutually accessible copy exists before dispute; and (4) the claimant cannot ordinarily reconstruct the missing links before discovery. When those conditions coincide, the design implication is *record reciprocity*: the institution should create a minimally sufficient account and make the transaction-facing portion accessible to the claimant, without altering the underlying liability standard. This is a design principle, not an independently enforceable right. Disability fair-housing rights have this structure with unusual force.

The deficit is one of *feature verification*: the primary missing substrate is transaction- and asset-level verification, with pattern analysis a secondary use of standardized records, not a substitute for them. Federal fair-housing data systems ask whether groups are distributed unequally across jurisdictions, neighborhoods, lending markets, or subsidized programs, and were built primarily for that question;[^13] the provisions examined here instead ask whether a particular request received a particular response in a particular timeframe, whether a specific building meets seven specific standards, whether a specific project clears the § 8.22(b) accessible-unit floor — questions pattern data cannot answer.[^14] The distinction has a substantial scholarly pedigree.[^15] Prior work diagnosed the deficit; this Note adds a sequence. Where operative facts arise in ordinary administration, record production precedes later investigation, pleading, and adjudication: the repeat-player literature explains who can navigate enforcement, while record-dependence asks what transaction- and asset-level evidence must exist before any actor can do so. That sequence is why the remedy begins upstream, with administrative record creation and claimant access, rather than downstream, with a new liability rule. Nor is the diagnosis a relabeled burden-allocation account: penalty-default rules and burden-shifting devices induce disclosure of proof a party already possesses, while record-dependence identifies rights whose operative facts are never created in preserved, linked, claimant-accessible form — a burden shift cannot reach a record that does not exist.

## A. The Enacted Disability Package

The 1988 disability package created rights whose ordinary administration requires records. The FHAA added three feature-verification duties: § 3604(f)(3)(A), reasonable modifications at the requester's expense; § 3604(f)(3)(B), reasonable accommodations necessary to afford equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling;[^19] and § 3604(f)(3)(C), implemented through 24 C.F.R. § 100.205, seven design-and-construction requirements for post-March-13-1991 covered multifamily housing.[^21]

HUD's 24 C.F.R. Part 8 implements § 504 of the Rehabilitation Act[^151] and imposes a 5% mobility-accessibility floor (plus 2% hearing-vision) on new-construction and substantial-alteration projects under § 8.22(b).[^22] Section 8.24 imposes a program-accessibility standard on existing stock "when viewed in its entirety";[^23] § 8.26 requires accessible units to be distributed throughout projects and sites; § 8.27 requires suitable means to ensure they reach those who need their features; and ADA Title II independently binds PHAs as public entities, supplying a parallel program-accessibility standard.[^24] Sections 8.22 and 8.24 establish *capacity*, §§ 8.26–8.27 *placement*, and § 8.4(d)'s most-integrated-setting mandate states the anti-segregation norm *Olmstead* enforces under Title II — the norm remains judicially applied and the HUD provision codified, though the Executive Branch now deems its HHS and DOJ counterparts beyond statutory authority, and no module of Part III's proposed rule depends on § 8.4(d).[^25][^176] Each duty presupposes a record — inventory, placement, matching queue — and no identified coordinated federal reporting system supplies those records.[^26]

## B. Section 504's Missing Compliance Baseline

Section 504's record dependence is explicit — and, by design, time-boxed: § 8.51 required a self-evaluation within one year of July 11, 1988; § 8.24(d), transition plans within six months; § 8.25(c), PHA needs assessments within two years.[^145] Nothing requires those documents to be updated or reported recurringly; the regulation's administrative memory was designed to be created once and to age. One provision is different in kind: § 8.55(b)'s recordkeeping and compliance-report authority is continuing — the reason Part IV.D treats it as a candidate implementation vehicle. The clearance record confirms the obsolescence: the supporting collection — OMB Control No. 2529-0034, still displayed in Part 8's text — lapsed at the end of 1996; the sole Federal Register mention since is a June 2001 reinstatement proposal never completed; and no identified successor appears in the located public record.[^145] Under the PRA's public-protection provision, a demand lacking a valid control number ordinarily cannot be enforced through penalties.[^128] The lapse did not repeal Part 8 or rescind § 8.55 — but it left recurring, portfolio-wide use of Part 8's own reporting machinery without the clearance enforceable collection presupposes.

## C. Reasonable Accommodation as the Paradigm Record-Dependent Duty

A prima facie claim requires a qualifying disability, provider notice, a specific request, a disability-related nexus, necessity, reasonableness, and denial or unreasonable delay.[^28] Custody of the underlying facts is distributed — disability, nexus, and necessity documented by the claimant or a physician; receipt, routing, timing, stated reason, and decisional authority provider-side — and no single contemporaneous record, mutually accessible to both sides, ordinarily captures the exchange. The gaps are outcome-determinative: *Schwarz v. City of Treasure Island* held that an accommodation claim fails where the provider was not on notice, adopting *Keys Youth Services*' principle; *Ely* applied it.[^29][^30] A tenant may have a disability-related need and a provider may have refused something necessary to equal use and enjoyment, yet the claim may still fail because the request was not communicated as notice, the necessity not articulated as nexus, or the response not preserved as denial. Fair-housing organizations and disability-rights counsel construct the records that make these claims cognizable — the record-creation function Part II.D develops.[^178]

## D. Existing Fragments Prove Feasibility, Not Completion

Existing instruments reveal a fragmented baseline, not a blank slate. Form HUD-50058 contains Public-Housing-only accessibility-feature fields at lines 5e–5g.[^39] PIH Notice 2022-03 directs PHAs to record each unit's accessible designation in IMS/PIC under four categories.[^40] Form HUD-9834, the multifamily management-review instrument, carries a civil-rights addendum whose accessible-unit census counts the "[n]umber of persons on waiting list who have requested accessible units," forwarded to FHEO — while the instrument itself states that it "does not require the reviewer to make a determination of civil rights or Section 504 or ADA compliance."[^155] Multifamily waiting lists must record "the need for an accessible unit, including the need for accessible features" — but HUD's own sample format reduces the element to a Y/N column, and no duty requires the column ever to be counted, summarized, or transmitted.[^167] And the April 2025 HCV Guidebook describes model written procedures that "could include" information on how requests are made and handled — oral requests documented in writing, records maintained of all requests, a recommended requester copy: guidance, not obligation, and precisely the transaction record this Note proposes to standardize.[^156]

The condition of those instruments is the point. HUD's Inspector General found in March 2025 that civil-rights monitoring — full completion of the HUD-9834 addendum — occurred in only 12 of 132 sampled multifamily reviews from April 2021 through December 2023, roughly 9 percent.[^37] The complaint layer is reactive — records created after a dispute ripens, not provider-stage request logs, live inventories, or allocation queues.[^45] The inspection layer confirms the gap: on the author's source-based UFAS/§ 504 crosswalk, NSPIRE inspections reach only a minority of accessibility-requirement categories even in part, and no NSPIRE item counts accessible units.[^46][^47] HUD's own preamble acknowledged that "[c]ompliance with these NSPIRE Standards does not mean the participant has complied with the Federal accessibility standards," and the channel it assigns instead — FHEO review — is one GAO found barely exercised.[^49] A property scoring 100 on NSPIRE remains consistent with material § 3604(f)(3)(C) or § 504 noncompliance.[^50]

HUD's own oversight has already prescribed the missing pieces. GAO found in 2023 that HUD does not systematically collect or report usable data on accommodation requests, denials, or accessible-unit stock — yet estimated that more than 83% of surveyed PHAs already collect accessibility-needs information locally; HUD simply does not require centralized reporting, and both GAO recommendations remain open, the planned data-system enhancement delayed with no completion date.[^36] HUD's Inspector General recommended in 2022 that HUD "[r]equire that PHAs track requests for reasonable accommodation, including the date of the request, the type of request, and the disposition and date of any action taken";[^37] the recommendation was closed in January 2025 as a single-program oversight item.[^38] An impracticability objection is therefore overstated: the relevant fields already exist somewhere in HUD's own instruments — uneven and incomplete, but administrable.

## E. Part 121, § 3608(e)(6), and the Mandate–Instrument Mismatch

What HUD reports and what the statute names diverge in a checkable way. HUD publishes disability characteristics of assisted households,[^7] but § 3608(e)(6) directs annual public reporting on the handicap and family characteristics of persons and households "who are applicants for, participants in, or beneficiaries or potential beneficiaries of" HUD programs — and no identified recurring public reporting reaches the applicant and potential-beneficiary side of that universe, where waiting-list demand for accessible units sits. The divergence has a documented history: HUD's first-generation § 3608(e)(6) reports titled themselves "Applicants and Beneficiaries," and carried dedicated applicant and potential-beneficiary sections; the modern series retains the statutory recitation while reporting on beneficiaries alone.[^166] The 2022–2026 HUD-27061 sequence is the clearest current illustration of that mandate–instrument mismatch — an instrument described more broadly than it was built, on a record of HUD's own making — not the Note's load-bearing predicate.[^168]

Congress paired § 3614a's data parenthetical with § 3608(e)(6)'s annual reporting command.[^52] The reporting command is textually distinctive: Title VII obliges employers to report only "as the Commission shall prescribe by regulation or order" — while § 3608(e)(6) directs that the Secretary "shall" report annually and "shall, without regard to any other provision of law, collect such information relating to those characteristics as the Secretary determines to be necessary or appropriate."[^177] HUD implemented these authorities in Part 121 in January 1989; the preamble engaged commenters' assertion that HUD "has failed to generate such data" and responded that it "remains committed to that objective."[^53] Part 121's operative provision, § 121.2, directs program participants to furnish data concerning handicap and family characteristics as the Secretary may determine to be necessary or appropriate,[^54] and Part 121 is not dormant: HUD's Housing Counseling Program regulation conditions participating agencies' recordkeeping systems on Part 121 conformity.[^158] What the located record shows is fragmentation rather than use at scale: Part 121 is nearly absent from reported decisions and located federal enforcement records,[^141] and no recovered annual report across three decades discloses a Part 121 collection or cross-tabulates disability data with program records — over a span in which disability rose from 19% of complaint bases (FY 1989) to the modal protected class (60.4% by FY 2018).[^142]

The 2022 notice placed protected-class data and Part 121 in the administrative record; the 2023 notice declined to carry the announced expansion into the operative collection; and the 2026 record presents both branches at once — the June 12 notice proposes the narrowed form unchanged, its abstract still describing "race, ethnicity, and other protected class data" required by the Fair Housing Act, while the June 30 expiration arrived with no successor on record.[^6][^144] HUD-27061 could never itself carry request logs or unit inventories; the sequence matters because HUD invoked, then declined to carry forward, the protected-class data authority those records would operationalize — and the located public record does not disclose why the announced scope changed. HUD applied the same 8,625-hour burden estimate to both collections, with no burden rationale for the narrowing in the public record;[^55][^56] and neither the expiration nor the renewal need be the final agency action challenged — the 2022–2026 record completes the administrative predicate.[^153] A completed renewal would not moot this record; it would restate it, carrying the 2023 omission forward in a fresh PRA record. A lapse leaves enacted disability duties with no cross-program collection at all — though a gap between expiration and renewal may prove routine, which is why the argument does not depend on lapse. Neither branch supplies the companion architecture, and neither displaces the vehicle: the renewal's comment period addresses the form; the § 553(e) petition addresses the missing rule.[^153]

# PART II: PRIVATE ENFORCEMENT AND THE RECORD GAP

## A. Why the Private-Enforcement Fallback Falls Short

After the 2025–2026 administrative-withdrawal events — the 2025 AFFH interim final rule, the termination of seventy-eight Fair Housing Initiatives Program grants, the proposed disparate-impact rescission, and reported FHEO reductions — the fallback position has been that disability rights remain enforceable through the FHA's private right of action.[^60] That conclusion misunderstands the right: private litigation depends structurally on administrative infrastructure rather than replacing it. Disability fair-housing claims depend on records private plaintiffs often cannot create before discovery, and doctrinal limits foreclose some of the strongest remedies: *Cummings v. Premier Rehab Keller* eliminated emotional-distress damages in § 504 private suits,[^61] and in jurisdictions following *Garcia v. Brockway*, private § 3604(f)(3)(C) claims often expire two years after construction completion.[^62] The less visible problem is institutional: when the institutions that screen complainants, develop facts, and translate disability injuries into legally sufficient allegations contract, private enforcement does not absorb the slack — it reshapes the pool, leaving a docket increasingly composed of litigants who must reconstruct institutional records without institutional help.[^63] The vulnerability is structural, not tied to any single administration.

## B. Database and Methodology

The empirical analysis uses the FHA Unified Database, an original corpus of 1,900 screened federal opinion and order records assembled from CourtListener's REST API and coded with a multi-classifier pipeline whose reproducibility was tested in a separate audit.[^68] The corpus measures the decisional pipeline — federal disability fair-housing opinions in which courts generated a public legal record — not all violations, complaints, or settlements. Cases resolved by settlement, administrative disposition, voluntary dismissal, or unreported order sit outside the denominator, so nothing in this Part is a claim-success rate.[^66] Because the corpus conditions on retrievable opinions, the design does not identify the stage — filing, disposition, opinion production, or retrieval — at which any composition change arises; this Part does not estimate the causal effect of representation, missing records, or any proposed field, and does not measure the regulated population of Part III's rule.

The composition series uses three date-defined windows: P1 (January 1, 2022–June 27, 2024); P2 (June 28, 2024–February 4, 2025); P3 (February 5, 2025–the July 1, 2026 data endpoint), labeled by date rather than event to avoid implying causation.[^67] The outcome census is reported pooled because no difference between the substantive windows is detected — establishing neither decline nor equivalence. The composition claim rests on representation status as stated on the face of each opinion — a low-inference field, though machine-extracted (see *infra* note 75); outcome-rate point estimates depend on classification choices and are less certain.[^68] Validation proceeded in two stages — a reproducibility-based pipeline stage, reported as cross-classifier agreement rather than accuracy against a human-coded gold standard, and a case-level adjudication in which every identified decided case was read and its outcome-cell membership adjudicated individually — plus a blind sweep of related documents across the full document universe for missed judgments, whose measured under-call bounds are in the margin.[^68]

## C. The Composition Effect

A *qualifying judgment* is one of three events, separately counted in Table 1: a final contested judgment awarding relief, a final default judgment, or a liability determination entered with the remedy reserved. Across the full span, eighteen qualifying plaintiff-side judgments — nine contested, two default, seven liability-only — appear among 606 decided cases, counting each case once — roughly 3.0% of the captured decided corpus, a corpus-limited proportion rather than an enforcement success rate. In every one, counsel had appeared for the plaintiff by the qualifying disposition; none arose among the 400 identified pro se cases. Nor does the counseled set reflect late-arriving counsel: fifteen of the eighteen were source-verifiably filed by counsel, and the remaining three filing postures are archival unknowns, recorded as unavailable rather than imputed.[^164] The comparison is descriptive — it does not identify the effect of counsel, case selection, or record availability (Part II.F) — and it supports no trend claim in either direction. What did change is the pipeline's composition: the case-level pro se share of the decided corpus rose from 59.6% to 76.1%.[^75]

## D. Repeat Players as Substitute Record Infrastructure

Repeat players build records as well as know the law: they identify the disability nexus and the responsible actor, preserve request timing, and frame a modification as a statutory request rather than a generalized grievance — substitute administrative infrastructure, the record pro se plaintiffs are later faulted for lacking. But the substitution is partial: counsel can organize and translate an existing account; counsel cannot recreate provider-side receipt, routing, or decision records that were never made. Representation identifies whether counsel appeared and institutional status who sued; both therefore proxy record-development capacity — no causal "institutional effect" is estimated. Institutional and counseled cases reach merits adjudication more often than pro se cases in this corpus — a descriptive gate contrast; its counts and its exploratory estimates are preserved in the technical appendix.[^76]

## E. Pleading Gates and the Translation Problem

The same record-dependence problem appears under procedural labels — pleading specificity, § 1915 screening, prima facie failure. Each requires plaintiffs to allege institutional facts the housing system often has not preserved in accessible form. A pro se tenant may know that she asked for a transfer, told someone why, and heard nothing; the legally operative record asks who received the request, when, in what capacity, under what program, and with what response.

The pleading stage is the principal observed gate: source-confirmed pleading-stage defense wins account for at least 45% of decided P1 cases and at least 55% of decided P3 cases — lower bounds rounded down to the nearest five points as additional margin, because not every coded record includes a complete pleading-stage flag.[^140] How dismissals distribute across claim-specificity categories is not reported: the cells are too sparse and the specificity field failed blind validation.[^86] The pleading gate is where the record gap becomes doctrine: in this corpus, unrepresented litigants encounter this gate more often (the descriptive gate contrast of Part II.D), although the analysis does not identify why — and what the gate demands is translation of lived facts into the discrete elements a complaint must allege. That is the *translation problem* — the pleading-stage face of the record gap. Within the pleading-loss universe, coded dismissal rationales identify a recurring failure family — courts' stated grounds citing failures to allege notice, nexus, timing, denial, covered status, or feature specifics — appearing more often in pro se losses than represented ones; the coding classifies what courts said, not what claimants knew or possessed, and every layer is machine-based, with magnitude reported only in the margin.[^87] A record rule cannot plead a case; it can only preserve the institutional facts on which any later translation must operate.

*Ely* illustrates the mechanism: the legally operative record did not carry the disability nexus from the Board's May 2010 voucher decision into the July 2010 extension request. The regulatory hooks are already on the books: § 8.28 requires a PHA issuing a voucher to a family with a disabled member to provide a current listing of known accessible units, to weigh the difficulty of locating an accessible unit in voucher-extension decisions — the decision Ely's case reached — and, as revised in 2024, to apply exception payment standards as a reasonable accommodation. Duties of that specificity make contemporaneous records practically important.[^88]

## F. Causation Limits and Transition

The litigation evidence does not prove that HUD data collection would have changed any particular outcome; it shows something narrower — in the captured pipeline, enforcement turns on transaction-specific records, and litigants without counsel increasingly populate it. Nor is every missing fact institutionally held: disputes range from facts never translated into pleadable elements, to records providers hold, to records never created — with remedies ranging from translation assistance, to access duties, to Part III's creation and retention obligations.

The period boundaries bundle multiple shocks (*Loper Bright*, the February 2025 enforcement withdrawal, the FHIP terminations, the proposed disparate-impact rescission, ordinary docket variation) that the database cannot cleanly separate.[^89] One alternative — an across-the-board contraction of legal-services capacity — admits a partial test holding protected class constant: on the exploratory, machine-coded classification of courts' stated pleading rationales, the missing facts are disproportionately ones an institution holds, and they concentrate in record-dependent disability claims rather than in disparate-treatment or race comparators; the coded shares are preserved in the repository as exploratory.[^89] If counsel answered the same shocks with more conservative case selection, the small, entirely counseled judgment set would partly reflect it. Within-category case-quality selection remains unobserved; the registered selection audit is inconclusive on observed case-mix change and in any event does not identify the causal effect of representation or record infrastructure.[^90] Whether the proposed fields correspond, case by case, to the facts courts found missing is a record-custody question this corpus cannot answer alone; the composition it documents is context, not proof of field-level fit.

That is enough for the Note's administrative-law claim: the petition need not show that any missing field would have produced a plaintiff win — only that HUD's systems leave enacted duties without the records their administration requires, records no private plaintiff can reconstruct if they were never created. The composition findings enter the petition record as an important aspect of the problem for *State Farm* purposes. The next Part builds that floor.
# PART III: THE STATUTORY DATA FLOOR

HUD's program machinery already operates every element of this architecture — somewhere, for some program, in some instrument. For the Section 202 and 811 portfolios, HUD has codified an express civil-rights reporting duty — each owner "shall submit such reports as HUD may prescribe to demonstrate compliance with applicable civil rights and equal opportunity requirements" — alongside "a written, chronological waiting list showing the name, race, gender, ethnicity, and date of each person applying for the program," an enumeration that omits disability, though in a categorically eligible portfolio (Section 811 serves persons with disabilities by definition) the probative gap is accessibility-need data rather than disability status.[^169] Section 8.55(b) supplies a continuing compliance-reporting hook (Part IV.D), and the instruments surveyed in Part I.D carry accessible-unit fields today. The defect is fragmented implementation rather than absent machinery; this Part maps the authority to coordinate it, module by module along the record-dependence chain.

## A. The Authority Stack — Field by Field

Post-*Loper Bright*, the question is whether the statute authorizes the rule.[^91] The limiting principle is *verification without substantive expansion*: each field must be reasonably necessary to administer a preexisting duty, and the architecture may impose recordkeeping obligations but may not alter any liability standard, create an exhaustion requirement, or condition a claimant's rights on use of a prescribed form. Section 3614a expressly authorizes "rules for the collection, maintenance, and analysis of appropriate data";[^92][^93] it was enacted in the same 1988 statute as § 3604(f)(3)'s disability duties.[^94] Section 3608(e)(6) supplies person-level reporting authority for "handicap" alongside other enumerated characteristics,[^95] and § 3608(f) enumerates § 504 among the authorities whose coverage defines that reporting duty.[^96] Section 504, Part 8, § 3535(d), and program-specific contract authorities supply the assisted-portfolio hooks.[^97] Section 3608a is excluded; its current text applies to USDA.[^98]

Each category carries a principal unresolved limit. For aggregate counts, it is Privacy Act release rules and "necessary or appropriate" discretion. For transaction records, the risk of a process-regulation characterization (*Garcia v. Brockway* limits private remedies, not data). For inventories, § 3608(e)(6)'s person-level rather than building-level reach (§ 3614a supplies the FHA-covered-stock hook). And for unit-to-household matching, its dependence on program-contract authority and Privacy Act system-of-records treatment — regulator-only, the highest privacy and anti-classification stakes, the category a petitioner should be willing to drop.

The architecture is three expressly severable modules, ordered by proximity to the transactions claimants live through, plus the restricted matching record, whose individualized disability-linked matching most resembles the classifications now under federal challenge (Parts III.E, IV.D). *Module 1*, accommodation and modification transaction records with a contemporaneous claimant copy, is the core: its provider-side duty rests on § 504, Part 8, § 8.55(b), and PHA program authority, with § 3614a — sufficient even on its narrowest reading to support HUD's own collection, maintenance, and analysis of the resulting records — paired with the duties verified; it standardizes a process HUD's own guidance already describes, while carrying the greatest process-characterization and PRA complexity. *Module 2*, accessible-unit inventories and program-compliance records, rests on § 504, Part 8, § 8.55, and program instruments. *Module 3*, aggregate applicant, participant, beneficiary, and potential-beneficiary reporting, rests on § 3608(e)(6) and Part 121 — the strongest text, with the applicant side the portion no identified recurring reporting performs (Part I.E). Each module carries its own vehicle — program form, recurring § 8.55 report, PRA-cleared collection or pilot, contract term, or rulemaking — and a lower-burden vehicle narrows the dispute. A grant of Module 1 alone would materially advance the petition's central object; the matching record is dispensable: HUD could decline it while granting everything else. Recordkeeping is not automatically ministerial, but the duties here are already enacted,[^129] and fields tracking operationally specific text — § 3604(f)(3)'s seven design elements, § 3608(e)(6)'s enumerated categories, § 3614a's express data clause — are the most likely to survive independent review.[^111][^112]

## B. Minimum Viable Data Categories and Privacy Architecture

The perimeter is narrow: HUD-assisted housing only — programs where HUD conditions assistance through Annual Contributions Contracts, Housing Assistance Payments contracts, or comparable instruments — attaching to PHA administration rather than private voucher landlords, and excluding private-market stock, standalone LIHTC projects, and voucher-assisted tenancies in unassisted buildings. The first phase is PHA-administered programs — where the program relationship, the § 8.28 duties, and current guidance already locate the transaction; extension across the remaining assisted portfolio is a phasing question the petition leaves to HUD. Part II's corpus is deliberately broader than this perimeter, so the empirical record motivates the rule without measuring its regulated population; the archived perimeter census and its mandatory disclosures are in the repository.[^181]

The minimum categories: request category; request and response dates; grant/denial/delay disposition; accessible-unit count; accessibility type (the four categories already operational in PIH 2022-03 and Form HUD-50058[^99]); bedroom size; covered-building flag; Part 8 allocation preference; transfer-queue or waiting-list indicator; and verification source. Timing data is critical — doctrine treats unreasonable delay as constructive denial, yet no identified federal system records the underlying intervals.[^100] The privacy exclusions are explicit: no diagnoses or medical narratives, no public tenant-unit matching, no standalone assistance-animal registry, no functional-limitation narratives. Because program definitions of disability differ from the civil-rights definitions, the transaction record classifies observable acts — the provider's own treatment of a request as disability-related, or the requester's designation of it as such — rather than collecting a generalized disability status,[^165] and any unit-accessibility designation carries its source, standard, and date. For § 3604(f)(3)(C) design-and-construction compliance, these fields index covered status and accessibility type; they do not themselves verify the seven design features — a feature-level verification record is a petition-stage design question, not part of the proposed minimum.

Module 1 operationalizes the bounded reciprocity principle stated in Part I. It standardizes what HUD guidance already describes.[^156] The module imposes three obligations. The provider — the party the substantive duties already bind — creates and retains the record under two triggers, its own classification or the requester's designation through any accessible channel, so the logged universe is not left to the provider's coding choices and recurring mismatch between channels is itself an audit signal. The claimant receives a durable, contemporaneous transaction receipt — date received, responsible unit, request category, disability-related treatment (without diagnosis), status, disposition, the stated reason where one was recorded or is independently required, and any review contact. The regulator receives only the aggregate layer. The record is *dual-source and append-only*: the claimant's account is preserved separately from the provider's classification, the provider cannot overwrite it by selecting a code or narrow the record by declining to classify, and later corrections append to, rather than replace, the original entry.

The safeguards run in both directions. First, anti-formalism: no form or phrasing is required — an oral or informal request triggers the logging duty through either channel, so the record cannot become a second pleading standard. Second, no presumption and no safe harbor: an unlogged request supports no inference of non-making, a facially compliant log is not a defense, and no record failure defeats any notice, nexus, waiver, timeliness, or remedy theory; independent evidence remains fully available. Recordkeeping failures carry administrative consequences — funding-instrument enforcement and corrective-action process — not evidentiary ones. Third, a categorical code cannot substitute for an actual reason where other law requires reasons, and nothing alters accrual, limitations, exhaustion, or any substantive burden. Fourth, recorded information may be used adversely to the claimant only under independent legal authority for a documented, relevant purpose — a safeguard whose residual exposure is custody, not content: the record fixes only a fact the provider already possesses, but fixes it in provider and PHA files that state public-records law, eviction discovery, and third-party process can reach on their own authority, so the petition should address custody in terms — retention limits, access controls, an internal-use restriction carried through the funding instrument, and a requester election governing the durable copy — rather than through the Privacy Act treatment that governs only the regulator tier. Fifth, metrics pair disposition with timing — grant rates without intervals would conceal constructive denial[^100] — and no count or rate is a standalone compliance score. The design also separates acknowledgment from disposition — the record of receiving a request is not the record of deciding it — a decomposition the petition develops in full. The receipt is in the requested rule and normatively central, but it is a design option for HUD's consideration, not an asserted statutory minimum. Disclosure proceeds in three tiers: public aggregate fields with small-cell suppression, restricted regulator-only fields (including any matching fields), and confidential individualized documentation that never leaves the provider file.

The precedents begin inside HUD: its own guidance describes the request-documentation practice;[^156] its Housing Counseling Program conditions recipient recordkeeping on Part 121 conformity;[^158] and its 2019 Los Angeles Voluntary Compliance Agreement required accessible-unit procedures and § 504 recordkeeping citywide.[^108] HUD has prescribed the same architecture where fragmented records impaired its own oversight: an April 2023 letter of findings and resulting compliance agreement required a standardized accommodation log — request, oral-or-written form, dates, disposition, denial justification, completion, and appeals.[^170] Beyond HUD, HMDA supplies a public-versus-restricted disclosure analogy;[^102] Regulation B's adverse-action notice and IDEA's prior written notice show a requester-facing record supplementing substantive rights without becoming an element of the claim;[^162] and recurring disability-compliance collection is administrable at scale across comparable federal § 504 agencies, even as Part 8 has never been comprehensively revised since 1988. The HHS Part 84 update, once this list's most natural fourth member, is now double-edged — administrability evidence still, durability evidence no longer.[^173][^104][^106] Governance particulars belong to the rulemaking or clearance process;[^101] the comparators are administrability evidence, not interpretive authority.

## C. HUD-27061 Is Evidence, Not Remedy

HUD-27061, a demographic intake form, cannot carry the disability-data collection by itself: request, timing, inventory, and allocation records require companion records across program systems.[^6][^144] The form's existing confidentiality language — that reported information "will not be held confidential" and may be released under FOIA — is itself incompatible with the tiered privacy treatment disability data requires.[^103] The petition may ask HUD to restore or amend HUD-27061, but its core demand should be a disability-data rule that survives the lapse of any one form.

## D. Disability Data as a Floor, Not a Substitute

The petition begins with disability-feature verification rather than a general protected-class data rule: disability-feature data should not rise or fall with the political fortunes of demographic monitoring, and the remedy does not ask disability data to replace race data; the rule is a floor, not a ceiling. Because the rule is anchored in disability, whose records § 3604(f)(3), § 504, and Part 8 themselves define, it answers the *Feeney* concern even where racial distributional consequences follow.[^130] The strongest design objection is structural: disability-centered enforcement could harden "separate but accessible" patterns unless paired with integration constraints — §§ 8.26–8.27, Title II, and, while operative, § 8.4(d)'s most-integrated-setting provision. After the June 2026 OLC opinion, integration is a design constraint on the record system, not the rule's foundation. The no-safe-harbor principle (Part III.B) survives whatever becomes of any single integration provision; if § 8.4(d) were rescinded — or the 2026 evenhanded-treatment reading of § 504 extended beyond the integration mandate to Part 8's affirmative floors — the modules keyed to statutory duties would retain their functions untouched (transaction records administer § 3604(f)(3) requests; aggregate fields discharge § 3608(e)(6)), inventories would verify the § 8.22(b) floor and §§ 8.26–8.27 placement so long as those provisions govern — and the inventories would remain the only instrument able to show whether accessible units cluster, because no concentration analysis can proceed without the underlying records.[^132][^176]

## E. Records, Not Reports

The EEOC's July 2026 proposal to rescind the EEO report series is the strongest current statement of the case against recurring protected-class collection — burden, loose tailoring to enforcement, and the constitutional sensitivity of demographic classification — and it is best read not as an omen for this proposal but as a map of its joints.[^175] Four information functions travel under the label "data." *Transaction records* are generated by an event and preserved by the party the substantive duty already binds. *Asset records* describe regulated things — units, features, standards — and disclose nothing about any person. *Aggregate reports* are recurring transmissions to the regulator or the public — the only function resembling the EEO series. *Investigatory demands* are case-specific. Title VII itself enumerates the first family separately from the third — records made and kept, then preserved, then reports made "therefrom"[^177] — and the proposed rule is built on the same joints — transaction records in Module 1, asset records in Module 2, aggregate reporting in Module 3, the restricted matching record as the regulator-only program layer — each module with its own authority, vehicle, and severability (Part III.A).

The Commission-approved text now draws that separation inside a single regulatory part: it would rescind the EEO-1 through EEO-6 filings and "the recordkeeping and record preservation requirements related to these reports" — while expressly declining to rescind the part's "general record preservation requirements," provisions whose own text preserves "requests for reasonable accommodation" among ordinary personnel records, and while leaving charge-related preservation untouched.[^175] What the strongest current federal case against recurring demographic reports leaves standing, in a regulator's own text, is evidence that the two functions are separable in practice — and retention presupposes records already created without commanding their creation, which is what a housing transaction-record rule would add. An agency persuaded by every one of the EEOC's concerns could still administer claimant receipts and unit inventories: a receipt showing that a housing authority received an accommodation request is not a demographic report, and a list of which units have mobility features is not a registry of persons.

Module 3 cannot borrow the other modules' modesty: it is a recurring protected-class collection and must answer the EEOC-form objection directly. Its first answer is statutory: the EEO reports rest on Title VII's permissive footing — which is why the Commission can propose to prescribe none — while § 3608(e)(6) commands an annual report on enumerated characteristics, handicap among them, across applicants, participants, beneficiaries, and potential beneficiaries.[^177] Its second answer is design: the aggregate tier publishes suppressed compliance counts and timing measures — the timing and disposition aggregates riding Module 1's § 3614a authority, while § 3608(e)(6) supplies the characteristic layer — not diagnoses or person-level disclosures. HUD retains real discretion over fields, collection periodicity, suppression, and vehicle — the statute commands a report, not this Note's preferred one — but it forecloses treating recurring protected-class reporting as optional. The distinction also disciplines denial: a reasoned denial must engage the petition presented (Part IV.C), and an objection sounding in demographic-reporting policy answers Module 3 without answering a claimant receipt, an inventory field, or a restricted program record; a denial treating the four functions as one "data" project would reproduce, in the denial itself, the conflation this Part is designed to eliminate.

# PART IV: PETITION, ADMINISTRATIVE RECORD, AND CONTINGENT REVIEW

## A. The § 553(e) Petition and Its Administrative Predicate

Section 553(e) is an implementation vehicle for the minimum-record principle, not the source of that principle. It gives interested persons the right to petition for the issuance of a rule; HUD's Part 10 procedure extends that right across HUD's programs — including the assistance matters § 553(a)(2) would otherwise exempt — and requires the petition to set forth the substance of the proposed rule.[^115][^160] The petition's object is accordingly a defined rule, not a request for study: an amendment to Part 8 and the applicable program regulations, or a stand-alone companion regulation under § 3614a and Part 121. The rule would require covered PHAs and recipients to preserve the dual-source minimum record, with a contemporaneous accessible copy to the requester; to maintain accessible-unit inventory and program-compliance records; and to submit PRA-cleared aggregate counts, dispositions, and timing measures. Severably, and only under Privacy Act system-of-records treatment, it would maintain the restricted regulator-only matching records. Around that defined object, the petition invites — as alternatives, not relief § 553(e) compels — identification of the instruments already collecting each field, a published gap analysis, and lower-burden vehicles: a pilot, a common transaction-record specification, recurring § 8.55 reports, or program-form and contract revisions. It should also request module-by-module disposition — naming, for any module granted, the implementing instrument and schedule — so a grant identifies its vehicle rather than deferring it; a partial grant or a non-rule vehicle performing a module's function narrows the dispute to the modules denied. If HUD grants, HUD designs the implementation; if HUD denies, the court reviews the reasons, not the field list. A model petition is archived in the repository.[^115]

The 2022–2026 HUD-27061 sequence supplies the administrative predicate; the § 553(e) denial supplies the reviewable action. Direct PRA review would create avoidable complications over finality, reviewability, and defendant identity;[^128] the petition path is conventional — final agency action attaches once HUD denies, a conventional predicate under *Bennett v. Spear*;[^137] the defendant is HUD alone; and the remedy is remand for reasoned explanation.[^117] If HUD neither grants nor denies, § 555(b)'s duty to conclude the matter within a reasonable time supplies the bridge: a court may compel a response to a long-pending petition under § 706(1).[^148] Subsequent PRA action cannot dissolve the vehicle (Part I.E).

The petition does work whether or not litigation follows. It places a defined administrative record before the agency — materials a reasoned denial must engage in their principal aspects, not point by point. Those materials include § 121.2's text and the 1989 preamble commitment; the 2022–2026 notices and expiration record; the NSPIRE concession; §§ 8.22–8.27's duties; HUD's own fragments; the composition findings; and the privacy-and-phasing proposal. Even a denial would be, so far as the located record shows, HUD's first reasoned engagement with Part 121's disability categories since 1989. The § 706(1) unreasonable-delay backup is weaker and confined to the margin.[^138][^139]

## B. If HUD Denies: Standing, Review, and Remedy

This Section matters only if HUD denies without reasoned engagement and a plaintiff seeks review.

*Standing.* *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine* rejected standing grounded in an organization's costs of opposing agency action,[^119] and the Second Circuit applied the point in *Connecticut Fair Housing Center v. CoreLogic Rental Property Solutions, LLC*, dismissing the organization's appeal while reaching the individual co-plaintiff's merits.[^152] That asymmetry sets the design: an individual lead, not an organization. The best lead petitioner is a current disabled HUD-program participant or applicant with an open accommodation, transfer, or voucher-extension request, whose prospective access to assisted housing is administered through the functions the proposed records would make auditable.[^121] After *TransUnion*, the injury is not informational absence in the abstract but the petitioner's concrete, ongoing interest in the accessible-unit, accommodation, and transfer functions on which her housing depends.[^149] Traceability is met directly: the claim locates the *procedural violation* in HUD's own default — the denial of reasoned consideration, running from HUD to the petitioner in one step — while the *injury* remains the petitioner's concrete assisted-housing interest, on the procedural-rights track that relaxes the normal redressability and immediacy standards.[^150] The relief is modest: a nontrivial opportunity to obtain reasoned consideration, not a judicial command to promulgate a rule.

Three candid limits complete the analysis. First, adverse authority: *Gettman v. DEA* dismissed a petition-denial challenge for want of Article III injury,[^157] and the Federal Circuit in 2025 affirmed dismissal, again on standing, of an organization's challenge to a rulemaking-petition denial.[^159] The configuration here supplies what those petitioners lacked: an individual with a concrete, ongoing assisted-housing interest. Second, standing may be *module-specific*: the individual with an open transaction has the strongest claim to review of the transaction-record module's denial, and may not thereby obtain review of the aggregate module or the matching record — one more reason the petition is severable. Third, the strongest configuration is *recurrent* exposure: a current participant's ongoing program relationship presents redressability least dependent on any single transaction's timeline. No reported decision squarely validates this configuration; the theory — its traceability step included — is plausible but untested, and this Note presents it as such.

*Reviewability and remedy.* Three precedents bear adverse weight; none controls. *ADAPT v. HUD* rejected a generalized effort to compel HUD disability enforcement; this claim seeks reasoned disposition of a discrete rulemaking petition grounded in specific text.[^122] *Chaney* is distinguishable because the claim targets rulemaking, and *Massachusetts v. EPA* confirms that rulemaking-petition denials are reviewable;[^123] *Norton* is the closer question for § 706(1), but the petition operates through § 706(2)(A).[^124] Petition-denial review overturns denials only in "the rarest and most compelling of circumstances,"[^125] and relief is limited to explanation; the substantive choice remains the Secretary's.[^133][^134] The textual floor deserves candid concession: HUD's own rule promises an unsuccessful petitioner only denial "with a brief statement of the ground for denial," and § 555(e) asks no more — the operative discipline is § 706(2)(A) review of whether the reasons given engage the record.[^115]

## C. The Reasoned-Denial Floor: Coinbase, State Farm, and Architecture Mismatch

The likely result deserves honest statement: a competent denial resting on existing mechanisms, incremental development, privacy, burden, resource priorities, or uncertainty would probably survive review. *Coinbase* marks the explanation floor. "[W]hen an agency denies a petition for rulemaking, the record can be slim, but it cannot be vacuous": the denial must disclose a discernible path from the petition to the disposition, and a denial invoking workability doubts, competing priorities, or an incremental alternative must explain how those considerations answer the petition actually submitted.[^171] Above that floor the lawful domain is wide: in the distinct setting of direct Federal Circuit review under 38 U.S.C. § 502, *McKinney* illustrates a denial that cleared it.[^171] The claim here is accordingly strongest only where a denial materially misdescribes an existing mechanism, treats a partial instrument as complete, rests on an impermissible statutory premise, or passes over a severable low-burden request without explanation. That concession is deliberate: even a denial that clears the floor produces what no enforcement posture has yet required of HUD — a reasoned, public accounting on a defined administrative record, itself a record-creation event.

What the floor requires is module-specific. HUD's strongest answer to the transaction-record module is an incremental pilot invoking unstable event definitions and burden uncertainty; because HUD's own forms, guidance, and post-2019 enforcement instruments already specify the core dates, dispositions, reasons, and requester copies, a denial invoking further study must identify the uncertainty that matters and explain why those instruments do not resolve it. A denial of the inventory module resting on program heterogeneity must engage the proposal's source-standard-date design, which preserves rather than erases program differences. A denial of the aggregate-reporting module resting on 24 C.F.R. § 100.202(c) must identify the precise obstacle, since the module relies on the uniformly offered inquiries that rule permits, excludes diagnosis, and follows HUD's own earlier reporting model.[^165] A fourth answer is that existing planning mechanisms already suffice. AFFH planning, however, does not generate unit-level accessibility records, request histories, response-time fields, or Part 8 allocation records; treating it as adequate without explaining how it supplies the records the statutes presuppose would lack *State Farm*'s rational connection between record and decision — and Part I.D makes misdescription of HUD's own instruments a checkable, record-based defect.[^118] The 2026 posture sharpens the mismatch: on neither the June 30 expiration nor the pending narrowed renewal can HUD defend disability noncollection by pointing to the form.

## D. HUD's Strongest Defenses

*Enforcement discretion.* The petition asks HUD to decide whether to initiate rulemaking, not to bring enforcement actions, and *Chaney* does not bar review of rulemaking-petition denials where a procedural mechanism requires a reasoned response — supplied here by statute for § 3614a rules and, for assistance matters, by HUD's binding Part 10 commitment paired with § 555(e)'s congressional response duty (*see supra* note 160).[^126] The remaining threshold defenses — finality, AFFH adequacy, private-enforcement adequacy — restate questions answered above (Parts IV.A, IV.C, and II respectively). Part 121's "necessary or appropriate" qualifier preserves discretion but does not foreclose reasoned-explanation review of a category HUD invoked and never carried forward. The quieter evasion is a nominal grant naming no vehicle; the module-by-module disposition request (Part IV.A) is drafted against it, and § 706(1) remains the concededly weak residual for inaction dressed as action.

*Section 8.55 already authorizes compliance reporting.* Section 8.55(b) directs each recipient to keep records and submit "timely, complete, and accurate compliance reports" in the form the responsible civil rights official determines necessary, and contemplates that recipients "should have available for the Department data showing the extent to which individuals with handicaps are beneficiaries" of assisted programs.[^145] Section 8.55 is therefore not merely an objection but a candidate implementation vehicle. But it has operated case by case, through a compliance-review channel GAO found barely exercised;[^49] its displayed clearance expired in 1996 with no identified successor; and recurring use would require a current PRA clearance and program design HUD has never undertaken. A recurring § 8.55 compliance baseline would perform the module's function and narrow the dispute; what HUD cannot do is invoke § 8.55's existence while declining the clearance and program design its recurring use requires.

*Privacy, cost, and burden.* Part III.B's tiered disclosure publishes aggregate compliance facts, not medical records; the petition seeks a phased, assisted-housing pilot; and HUD's own burden accounting did not move when the collection's announced scope narrowed — the 2022, 2023, and 2026 notices used the identical 8,625-hour estimate, evidence about the 2022–2026 record rather than a burden estimate for the petition's modules, whose pricing is the ICR process's function.[^56] A burden-based denial must identify the burden it means, module by module. The strongest record-based objection is HUD's own: FHEO told GAO that aggregate request data would be resource-intensive without meaningful impact — but GAO answered on the same pages,[^109] so a denial repeating the FHEO objection without engaging GAO's response would perpetuate, not answer, the engagement deficit.[^154]

*Major questions, Pennhurst, and the PRA.* The proposal falls on the ordinary-administration side: a HUD-funded perimeter, categorical compliance facts, no private-market mandate, no public tenant-unit registry, § 3614a's express data clause anchoring authority. *See supra* note 102. The substantive duties are not new; the reporting obligation would be, but prospectively only — after notice-and-comment rulemaking, through future funding instruments, with no retroactive penalty and no new private right of action, existing remedies untouched.[^129] The § 3507(d)(6) bar insulates OMB's decisions to approve or not act upon a collection, not HUD's reasons for declining rulemaking.[^128]

*Section 504 authorizes only evenhanded treatment.* The June 2026 OLC opinion supplies this defense's current form: reading *Southeastern Community College v. Davis*, it concludes that § 504 requires "the evenhanded treatment of qualified handicapped persons" rather than affirmative obligations, and that the HHS and DOJ integration regulations exceed the statutes they implement.[^173] The answer is scope, then severance: the opinion addresses the integration mandate as applied to state institutionalization of persons with mental disabilities, and analyzes neither the FHAA, § 3614a, § 3608(e)(6), nor HUD's program-contract authority.[^173] Nor do the modules impose an affirmative substantive duty of the kind the opinion targets: they record the administration of duties already enacted, altering no liability standard (Part III.A). A denial extending the opinion to recordkeeping would owe an explanation of why documenting the processing of a § 3604(f)(3) request — a duty no party contests — falls together with a contested integration mandate no module invokes. And the severance answer must reach § 504 and Part 8 in terms: Module 2's inventories verify Part 8 duties the opinion nowhere analyzes (the § 8.22(b) floor, §§ 8.26–8.27 placement, § 8.28's voucher-stage duties); extending the evenhanded-treatment reading to those floors would take the step the opinion itself reserves.

*Recurring protected-class reporting is burdensome, untailored, and constitutionally sensitive.* The EEOC's July 2026 rescission proposal supplies this defense's strongest current form; the answer is Part III.E's records/reports distinction — which the proposal itself embodies by retaining the general preservation provisions reaching reasonable-accommodation requests while ending the reports.[^175] Modules 1 and 2 and the restricted matching record sit outside the objection's premise: they preserve events, assets, and restricted program records, require no generalized disability-status field, and publish no person-level information. Module 3 alone is a recurring report, and a HUD denial importing the EEOC's theory would need to explain why a mandatory reporting statute yields the same conclusion as a permissive one, and why tiered, suppressed, diagnosis-free aggregate fields do not answer the tailoring and classification concerns.

# CONCLUSION

HUD has had express statutory authority to require disability-related program-participant data since 1988 and regulatory text identifying the categories since 1989. What it lacks is not every record but the chain: existing instruments create portions of the relevant record — fragmented, episodic, weakly linked, inaccessible to claimants, never aggregated into recurring cross-program visibility. The 2022–2026 HUD-27061 sequence makes the pattern legally salient in a single record HUD itself created.

The litigation record shows the enforcement setting in which that pattern matters. In the captured opinion-bearing pipeline, eighteen qualifying plaintiff-side judgments (nine contested, two default, seven liability-only) appear among 606 decided cases across four and a half years; counsel had appeared by every qualifying disposition, and no unrepresented plaintiff obtained one — while the case-level pro se share rose from roughly 60% to 76%. Those facts describe the captured corpus, not why cases entered or left it; they leave private enforcement unestablished as an adequate substitute for administrative visibility.

The vehicle is a modular § 553(e) petition for a defined minimum-record rule, every module severable, implementation vehicles left to HUD. Its value is primarily administrative: a public accounting no enforcement posture has ever required HUD to produce; if HUD denies without engagement, arbitrary-and-capricious review supplies a contingent, deferential backstop — remand for explanation, not judicial design of a data system. The federal retreat of 2026 does not weaken the case for that accounting; it disciplines the design. A request receipt, a unit inventory, and an annual statutory report serve different duties and rest on different authorities; treating them as a single "data" project would invite all three to fall with the most contested. Here each function stands on its own footing, and the contested integration mandate carries none of them.

Donavette Ely's file contained the disability-related fact that made her later request legally salient; no system carried that fact forward in a form that counted when it mattered. The bounded lesson is record reciprocity: where an institution shapes the operative account, it should preserve a minimally sufficient, claimant-accessible record. For record-dependent rights, that record is not bureaucracy. It is the condition of enforcement.
